AI transformation rarely fails because of the technology. It fails when the organisation has not changed, only the tool.

Expanded version of a LinkedIn post · September 9, 2026

Short version →

AI transformation rarely fails because of the technology. A RAND Corporation report cites an estimate that more than 80% of projects fail for lack of alignment with the organisation, not for lack of a tool. A real transformation touches three things, in this order: who decides, which processes get redesigned, which roles genuinely evolve. The sign that a transformation has not happened: the number of approval meetings has not changed. The starting point fits on a single page: map who will need to decide differently once the tool is in place. If the answer is "no one," the project automates what already exists; it transforms nothing.

During my years alongside senior executives, I saw many tools and transformation plans come and go. AI did not yet exist in its current form. But the script has not changed.

The quality of the tool is rarely the issue. The real subject is what stays the same around it.

This same pattern has repeated at every technology wave: ERPs in the 2000s, then CRMs, more recently the cloud. Each time, the tool delivered on its technical promises. Each time, a share of the organisations that adopted it captured only a fraction of the promised value, because nothing else around it had moved.

A RAND Corporation report cites an estimate that more than 80% of AI projects fail, not for lack of technology, but for lack of alignment with the company’s priorities and organisation.

That figure does not say AI does not work.

It says a tool, on its own, transforms nothing.

🔧 Why is changing the software not enough?

Because the processes stay the same, the same roles, the same meetings where nobody really decides.

The result: AI is added to what already exists. It does not transform it.

The most reliable sign that a transformation has not happened: the number of approval meetings has not changed. A new tool that runs through the exact same sign-off chain as before, with the same people consulted in the same order, has not transformed anything, it has just sped up one link, not shortened the chain.

Have economists already named this pattern?

This paradox is not unique to AI. Economist Robert Solow noted it back in 1987, about the computerisation of business: "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." Researchers Erik Brynjolfsson and Lorin Hitt later showed why: technology investment only produces a measurable gain once it is matched by an equivalent organisational investment, one that rarely shows up in a budget line, redesigning processes, redistributing decisions, evolving roles.

Generative AI is reproducing the same pattern today, only faster. The tool installs itself in a matter of weeks. The organisation takes months or years to catch up, if it catches up at all. That gap between the two speeds is exactly what the estimate cited by RAND Corporation illustrates: only around one project in five would close it successfully.

What this means concretely for a mid-sized company’s leader

In a mid-sized company, this has a direct consequence: an AI project’s budget should never stop at the cost of the licence or the technical integration. Part of it needs to cover the time it will take to revisit, with the teams involved, who decides what once the tool is in place. That time never appears on a supplier invoice, yet it is exactly what determines whether the project joins the four out of five that fail, or the one in five that succeeds.

A simple check, six months after rollout, tells you whether that transformation really happened: ask the team involved to describe their work process without mentioning the tool. If the description matches the old one almost word for word, with one extra step, the transformation has not happened. The software changed. The work did not.

🎯 What three things does a real AI transformation have to touch?

  1. 1. Who decides, and when.
  2. 2. Which processes are rethought, not just automated.
  3. 3. Which roles genuinely evolve.

Who decides, and when, is a bottleneck question: if a decision still waits on the same three approvals it always did, the tool has not changed anything upstream of it. Redesigning a process is different from automating it — automating a broken step just makes the mistake happen faster. And a role genuinely evolves when the time a tool frees up gets redirected toward judgment calls a person makes, not simply absorbed back into more of the same. Each of these three can fail on its own — automating without deciding differently, or deciding differently without any role changing — and either gap alone is enough to stop it counting as a transformation.

Without that, the organisation quickly slips back into its old habits, with one more tool. A tool changes what is possible. Only the organisation decides what becomes real.

In what order should you tackle these three points?

These three points have an order. Deciding who decides, and when, comes before redesigning a process: without clarity on the first question, no one knows which process deserves to be rethought first. Redesigning the process comes before evolving roles: a role does not change because someone announced it should, it changes because the actual work behind it has taken a different shape.

An organisation that starts with the third point, announcing that roles will evolve without having revisited the decision or the process first, rarely gets what it hoped for: a change in language, not a change in outcome.

Where should you start, concretely?

A concrete starting point: before any AI project, map not what the tool will do, but who will need to decide differently once it is in place. If the answer is "no one," the project automates the existing setup, it transforms nothing. That map fits on a single page, its brevity is exactly what makes it hard to dodge.

A quick test to see where your organisation stands

Three questions are usually enough to place an AI project on this scale. Has anyone, since rollout, made a decision they would not have made before, with the same authority as before? Has a process lost a step, rather than gained one? Has anyone seen their role redefined, not just their tool? If all three answers are no, the project stands a good chance of joining the four out of five that fail, according to the estimate cited by RAND Corporation, whatever the quality of the AI chosen.

This test does not replace a full diagnosis. Its advantage is that it takes ten minutes, needs no consultant, and immediately tells you whether the subject deserves closer attention. It is often the first question I ask when an executive shows me an AI project already under way: not "which tool did you choose," but "who at your company decides differently since it has been running."
