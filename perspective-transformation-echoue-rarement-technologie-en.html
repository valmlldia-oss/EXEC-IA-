AI transformation rarely fails
because of the technology.
It fails when the organisation
has not changed, only the tool.

Article developed from my LinkedIn post (9 September 2026)
Updated on 25 September 2026

AI transformation rarely fails because of the technology.

A RAND Corporation report cites an estimate that more than 80% of projects fail for lack of alignment with the organisation, not for lack of a tool.

A real transformation touches three things, in this order: who decides, which processes get redesigned, which roles genuinely evolve.

The sign that a transformation has not happened: the number of approval meetings has not changed.

The starting point fits on a single page: map who will need to decide differently once the tool is in place.

If the answer is "no one," the project automates what already exists; it transforms nothing.

During my years alongside senior executives, I saw many tools and transformation plans come and go. AI did not yet exist in its current form. But the script has not changed.

The quality of the tool is rarely the issue. The real subject is what stays the same around it.

This same pattern has repeated at every technology wave: ERPs in the 2000s, then CRMs, more recently the cloud. Each time, the tool delivered on its technical promises. Each time, a share of the organisations that adopted it captured only a fraction of the promised value, because nothing else around it had moved.

A RAND Corporation report cites an estimate that more than 80% of AI projects fail, not for lack of technology, but for lack of alignment with the company’s priorities and organisation.

That figure does not say AI does not work.

It says a tool, on its own, transforms nothing.

🔧 Why is changing the software not enough?

Because the processes stay the same, the same roles, the same meetings where nobody really decides.

The result: AI is added to what already exists. It does not transform it.

The most reliable sign that a transformation has not happened: the number of approval meetings has not changed. A new tool that runs through the exact same sign-off chain as before, with the same people consulted in the same order, has not transformed anything, it has just sped up one link, not shortened the chain.

Have economists already named this pattern?

This paradox is not unique to AI. Economist Robert Solow noted it back in 1987, about the computerisation of business: "You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics." Researchers Erik Brynjolfsson and Lorin Hitt later showed why: technology investment only produces a measurable gain once it is matched by an equivalent organisational investment, one that rarely shows up in a budget line, redesigning processes, redistributing decisions, evolving roles.

Generative AI is reproducing the same pattern today, only faster. The tool installs itself in a matter of weeks. The organisation takes months or years to catch up, if it catches up at all. That gap between the two speeds is exactly what the estimate cited by RAND Corporation illustrates: only around one project in five would close it successfully.

What does this mean concretely for a mid-sized company’s leader?

In a mid-sized company, this has a direct consequence: an AI project’s budget should never stop at the cost of the licence or the technical integration. Part of it needs to cover the time it will take to revisit, with the teams involved, who decides what once the tool is in place. That time never appears on a supplier invoice, yet it is exactly what determines whether the project joins the four out of five that fail, or the one in five that succeeds.

A simple check, six months after rollout, tells you whether that transformation really happened: ask the team involved to describe their work process without mentioning the tool. If the description matches the old one almost word for word, with one extra step, the transformation has not happened. The software changed. The work did not.

🎯 What three things does a real AI transformation have to touch?

  • Who decides, and when.
  • Which processes are rethought, not just automated.
  • Which roles genuinely evolve.

Who decides, and when, is a bottleneck question: if a decision still waits on the same three approvals it always did, the tool has not changed anything upstream of it. Redesigning a process is different from automating it: automating a broken step just makes the mistake happen faster. And a role genuinely evolves when the time a tool frees up gets redirected toward judgment calls a person makes, not simply absorbed back into more of the same. Each of these three can fail on its own: automating without deciding differently, or deciding differently without any role changing — and either gap alone is enough to stop it counting as a transformation.

Without that, the organisation quickly slips back into its old habits, with one more tool. A tool changes what is possible. Only the organisation decides what becomes real.

In what order should you tackle these three points?

These three points have an order. Deciding who decides, and when, comes before redesigning a process: without clarity on the first question, no one knows which process deserves to be rethought first. Redesigning the process comes before evolving roles: a role does not change because someone announced it should, it changes because the actual work behind it has taken a different shape.

An organisation that starts with the third point, announcing that roles will evolve without having revisited the decision or the process first, rarely gets what it hoped for: a change in language, not a change in outcome.

Where should you start, concretely?

A concrete starting point: before any AI project, map not what the tool will do, but who will need to decide differently once it is in place. If the answer is "no one," the project automates the existing setup, it transforms nothing. That map fits on a single page, its brevity is exactly what makes it hard to dodge.

How can you quickly tell where your organisation stands?

Three questions are usually enough to place an AI project on this scale.

  • Has anyone, since rollout, made a decision they would not have made before, with the same authority as before?
  • Has a process lost a step, rather than gained one?
  • Has anyone seen their role redefined, not just their tool?

If all three answers are no, the project stands a good chance of joining the four out of five that fail, according to the estimate cited by RAND Corporation, whatever the quality of the AI chosen.

This test does not replace a full diagnosis. Its advantage is that it takes ten minutes, needs no consultant, and immediately tells you whether the subject deserves closer attention. It is often the first question I ask when an executive shows me an AI project already under way: not "which tool did you choose," but "who at your company decides differently since it has been running."

Going further

Four more questions to take the thinking further.

How does generative AI differ from earlier waves?

On one point it changes the game: it comes in through individuals, not through leadership. An ERP or a CRM was rolled out from the top, with a project, a budget and a schedule. A generative AI tool can be downloaded in two minutes, without asking anyone.

Transformation therefore no longer starts from a plan. It starts from uses already present, often invisible, which leadership must first discover before it can steer them. The starting point is reversed, not just the speed.

Why does productivity often drop before it rises?

Because an organisation that changes how it works first pays a learning cost. Economists, including Erik Brynjolfsson, describe this as a J-curve: performance dips while teams learn and processes are reorganised, then rises above its starting level.

For a leader, knowing this curve avoids a common mistake: stopping a project at the bottom of the dip, just as the effort begins to pay off. Announcing the dip in advance, with its likely duration, also protects the teams’ trust.

Which process should you start with?

The one that combines four traits:
it comes round often, every day or every week;
it frustrates both those who live it and those waiting for its result;
its result is visible, to a client or to leadership;
its scope is limited to one or two teams.

A first success on a process of this kind is worth more than an ambitious project that gets bogged down. It creates proof, and proof creates the will to continue elsewhere.

Should you automate first, then transform?

Starting by automating a step is not a mistake, provided it is done knowingly. Automation lets you learn, measure and reassure.

The mistake is to stop there. Every automation should come with a written question: if this step goes faster, which decision or next step must change for the gain to reach the client? Without that question, the company piles up local speed-ups that never join together.

How do you manage the period when old and new coexist?

By giving it an end date. Running the old process alongside the new one is reassuring at first, then becomes the worst of both worlds: everyone does their work twice, and nobody knows which version counts.

A switch-over date, known to all, settles the matter. Before that date, the old process remains the reference. After it, only the new one counts, and the old one is withdrawn, including the files and shortcuts that would allow a quiet return.

An example: handling quotes in an SME

Before the project, a quote follows this circuit:
the salesperson gathers the information and drafts a first version;
the technical manager checks feasibility;
leadership approves the price;
the assistant formats and sends it.

An AI tool can prepare the first version in a few minutes. If the rest of the circuit does not change, the quote simply waits longer at the next step.

Transformation means asking, for example, whether leadership must approve every price or only those outside a defined grid, and whether the technical check can rely on a checklist prepared by the tool. The real gain comes from those two decisions, not from faster drafting.

Which new roles are emerging?

Not necessarily new jobs, but new responsibilities, which are better named than left vague.

Three come up often:
the use champion, who knows the tool and answers colleagues’ questions;
the reviewer, who checks what the tool produces before it leaves the team;
the process owner, who decides how the circuit evolves and arbitrates when two teams disagree.

These responsibilities take time. If they are added without anything being removed elsewhere, they are carried out poorly, then not at all.

Which skills should come first?

Less technical mastery than three underlying abilities: stating clearly what you expect, checking what you receive, and judging when the result falls short.

These skills are not new. They are those of a good delegator, who knows how to hand over a task, check the result and take back control when needed. Managers who already have them adapt quickly to AI tools; those who never developed them discover the problem on this occasion.

How do you measure that a transformation is progressing?

Four easy-to-track indicators say more than a complex dashboard:
the time between a request and the decision that answers it;
the number of steps in the processes concerned;
the share of teams actually using the new tools;
the time freed up, and what it is really spent on.

Recorded at launch and then every quarter, these figures tell the project’s real story far better than impressions gathered in meetings.

How do you avoid the project depending on one person?

Many transformations have an engine: an enthusiastic person who found the tool, convinced leadership and trained colleagues. The day that person changes jobs, the project dies out.

The remedy is to form a pair early, then a small group, sharing knowledge and decisions. Documenting what has been learned, the instructions that work and the mistakes to avoid, is part of the project just as much as the roll-out itself.

Should you tell clients?

Yes, as soon as the transformation changes something for them: a lead time, a contact person, a reply format. A client who has been warned accepts an adjustment; a client taken by surprise experiences it as a decline.

Asking for their view a few weeks after the switch also provides a measure internal indicators do not: has the change improved what they experience, or only what the company experiences?

How long should you allow?

Longer than rolling out the tool, and less long than feared if you proceed step by step. On a limited process, a few months are often enough to see the decision, the circuit and the roles genuinely shift.

What stretches timelines is rarely the technology. It is postponed decisions: who now approves, which step disappears, who takes over which task. Every week of delay is paid for in duplicated work and team fatigue.

What should you tell teams about what will not change?

It is often the most awaited question, and the least addressed. A transformation worries people because it seems to call everything into question.

Stating clearly what remains stable, the values, the commitments to clients, the people one reports to, gives a foothold to those who must change the rest. Change is easier to accept when it has known limits.

When should you bring in an outside view?

When the organisation can no longer see its own habits. Approval circuits, ritual meetings and implicit roles seem natural to those who live them every day.

An outside view does not bring a ready-made solution. It asks questions nobody asks internally any more, without any stake in power, and helps separate what is necessary from what is merely habit.

What transformation really demands

Leadership time, more than budget. Tools are bought quickly; decisions about who decides, which processes change and which roles evolve cannot be delegated.

Leadership that agrees to spend that time gets what the tool alone will never deliver: an organisation that works differently, not just faster.

How do you involve teams from the start?

→

By having them take part in describing the problem before the solution is mentioned. The people doing the work know the real sticking points, the ones no organisation chart shows.

A transformation designed with them is far more likely to be applied than one that is announced.

A half-day workshop, where each person describes a difficult task from their week, often brings out more leads than a questionnaire.

Invite a declared sceptic too: objections heard early cost far less than resistance discovered after launch.

How do you know an organisation has really changed?

→

Three concrete signs: decisions are made by other people or at another time, processes have actually been modified, and roles have evolved, with tasks abandoned.

If none of this has moved, the tool has been installed but the organisation has not changed.

These findings are easier to establish with someone outside daily routines, who asks naive questions with no power stake.

Ask the question again a few months later: real change stands the test of time, while cosmetic change fades as soon as attention drops.

What do you do when the transformation stalls?

→

Return to the starting question: what was supposed to change, and what is preventing it? The answer is almost always a decision not taken, a process not reviewed or a role not clarified.

Adding a new tool at this stage makes the problem worse: the organisational obstacle has to be removed first.

A short monthly checkpoint on the obstacle of the moment is enough to stop the blockage from settling in.

Name the obstacle in writing, with an owner and a date: a blockage nobody owns ends up being worked around rather than removed.

What role does the leader play over time?

→

Holding the course and making choices visible: what continues, what stops, what will not be done. A transformation lasts months, and the leader’s attention is one of its scarcest resources.

A leader who stays present without steering everything gives the organisation permission to change.

This checkpoint also works as a signal: it shows that the subject remains a leadership priority, not a project quietly fading.

In practice, a few minutes set aside for the topic in every executive committee meeting are enough to keep that signal alive.
