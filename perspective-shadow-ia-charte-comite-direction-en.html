An AI policy that its own
leaders do not follow
is no longer a policy.

September 25, 2026

Short version →

Executive committee members: according to a WalkMe study from July 2025, 78% of employees use AI tools their company has never approved.

A 2025 Cybernews survey adds that 93% of the executives and senior managers surveyed do the same.

To me, the issue is not team discipline. It is the governance around it.

Three reflexes: acknowledge real usage before banning it, offer safe and simple tools, look at the leadership team’s own habits first.

You sit on an executive committee and you have just adopted an AI policy?
This piece is for you.
The policy is drafted in committee. And in the lift afterwards, everyone goes back to their personal AI tool.

A WalkMe study from July 2025 confirms it at scale: 78% of employees use AI tools their company has never approved.
More surprising: according to a 2025 Cybernews survey, 93% of the executives and senior managers surveyed do exactly the same.

On the company side, Gartner reaches the same conclusion: 69% of organisations suspect or have evidence that their teams use unauthorised generative AI.

🔍 Why is shadow AI not a question of discipline?

Because usage is massive, and shared all the way to the top. When almost everyone works around the rule, it is no longer about individuals. It is a sign that the rule does not meet the need.
Punishing will not change anything. As long as the official tool is slower than the personal one, people will make their own choice, quietly.
So the right question becomes: what does the official tool not let people do?

The issue is not team discipline.
It is the governance around it.

An AI policy that its own leaders do not follow is no longer a policy. It is a shared fiction.

What does shadow AI actually hide?

Behind the expression, there are people who want to save time: summarising a document, rewording a message, preparing a meeting. Nothing malicious.
But all of it happens with tools the company has not assessed, and sometimes with information that should never leave the building.

So the risk is not the tool. It is the lack of a clear rule on what can be entrusted to it, and the lack of visibility for leadership.
As long as nobody knows who uses what, nobody can protect the data, or make the most of the good ideas already circulating.

Which data should never leave the company?

Customer information, contracts, personal data, non-public financials and anything covered by business secrecy. A useful policy says so in one simple sentence, with examples everyone recognises from their own work.

On the regulatory side, notably GDPR, an SME that uses AI will find its obligations set out in the AI Act and GDPR guide for an SME.

How do you map usage without hunting for culprits?
By asking in a way that does not punish: which tools save you time, and for which tasks?
A small-group conversation or a short anonymous questionnaire gives a more honest picture than a control exercise.

The goal is to bring real needs to the surface, then answer them with approved tools.

What are the concrete risks of shadow AI?

Three families of risk.
1) An information leak, when a confidential document is pasted into an uncontrolled tool.
2) A decision based on a wrong answer, never reviewed, that then circulates as a fact.
3) A compliance risk, when personal data is processed without a framework.

None of these risks requires malicious intent: they arise from an ordinary gesture, made to save time.

Why do bans so often fail?
Because they leave the need intact.
A person banned from a tool that helps them keeps looking for the same gain elsewhere, out of sight. A ban moves usage into the shadows instead of removing it.

A rule that offers a simple, safe alternative has a far better chance of being followed than one that merely forbids.

Why acknowledge real usage before banning it?

🎯 Which reflexes change the game?
To start, three are enough.
1) Acknowledge real usage.
2) Offer safe and simple tools.
3) Set the example at the top.

Because it already exists, whether we like it or not. Banning a use you do not know about pushes it into the shadows. Mapping it, without hunting for culprits, finally gives an accurate picture of what teams actually do.

How do you offer safe tools that are simpler than the ones people hide?
By providing approved tools that are as quick to pick up as personal ones. If the safe option is heavier than the hidden one, the hidden one wins.

Why start with the leadership team’s own practices?

Because consistency starts at the top. An executive committee that looks honestly at its own usage gives the policy a credibility that no internal memo can replace.

An organisation never secures what it refuses to look at.

What should a useful AI policy contain?
Five elements are often enough.
1) Authorised uses.
2) Forbidden data.
3) Approved tools.
4) A named person to ask questions.
5) A review date.

A two-page policy that everyone has read is worth more than a twenty-page document nobody opens.
It should also be written for those who use it, with their tasks and their words.

How often should it be reviewed?

Regularly, because tools and uses change fast. A quarterly rhythm, with a short item on the executive committee agenda, lets you add an approved tool, remove a use that became risky and check that the rule still matches practice.

A rule frozen for a year describes a situation that no longer exists.

How does a leader set the example?
By applying the policy personally, saying which tools they use and for what, and accepting that the question is put to them.
When executives do what they ask others to do, the rule stops being a constraint and becomes a shared norm.

That is the condition for the policy to stop being a fiction and become a steering tool again.

Where do you start this week?

With a half-hour conversation in the executive committee: which AI tools does each person really use, and with what information?
That frank, judgement-free conversation is worth more than a new version of the policy.

It provides the only solid basis for deciding what to allow, what to offer and what to forbid.

How do you choose which tools to approve?
Start from the real uses found in your company, not from a feature catalogue. Three questions help.
1) What happens to the information entered?
2) Who has access to it?
3) Can people get started with the tool in a few minutes?

It is better to approve a few well-explained tools than to draw up a long list nobody consults.

What role for the legal function or the delegated executive?

A supporting role, not a blocking one. The named contact answers questions quickly, keeps the list of approved tools up to date and alerts the executive committee when a new use appears. Without a fast answer, teams do without the opinion.

In a small organisation, this contact can be the leader personally, provided their availability is known to everyone.

How do you raise awareness without blaming anyone?
By talking about plausible cases rather than faults. A simple example, such as confidential meeting minutes pasted into a consumer tool, is enough to convey the stakes without singling anyone out.

Awareness works better when it comes from the executive committee itself, which acknowledges its own uses before asking others to change theirs.

What should you do about a use that is already in place?

Map it first, without judgement, then decide: keep it if it is safe, replace it with an approved tool if it is not, or frame it with a clear rule.
Plan a transition period so teams do not lose what saves them time.

Announce the change with its reasons. A use withdrawn without explanation almost always reappears elsewhere.

What should you do about information already shared with an unapproved tool?
Assess calmly what may have been shared, without looking for someone to blame. Depending on the nature of the information, notably if it contains personal data, seek legal advice before deciding what to do next.

Note what was learned and build it into the policy: it is the best way to prevent the situation from recurring.

Who should own this topic in the organisation?

A clearly named member of the executive committee, with an operational relay. The subject touches tools, data, human resources and compliance all at once. Without an owner, it is handled piecemeal, and nobody has the full picture.

This owner does not need to be an AI specialist. They must know how to ask the right questions and decide when two needs collide.
In an SME, it is often the leader or their right hand. What matters is that everyone knows who to turn to, and that this person answers quickly.

The same principle applies to every future tool. Each time a new AI service appears on the market, the question will come back: is it approved, for which uses, and with which data?
A policy that answers this question quickly, in writing and in plain words, does more for safety than a long document read once and forgotten. Keeping it short, current and visible is the real discipline, and it is a task that belongs to the leadership team, not only to the people who manage the tools.

What shadow AI costs, beyond the risks

People mostly talk about leaks and compliance. A quieter cost goes unnoticed: the know-how being built outside the company.

An employee who uses a personal tool every day builds up effective prompts, message templates, ways of working. All of it lives in a private account. The day they leave, that capital leaves with them, without anyone knowing it existed.

Add the scattered spending: individual subscriptions paid with a personal card or slipped into expense claims, often several for the same need. One by one, they look harmless. Added up, they fund an AI policy nobody decided on.

Finally, the company loses negotiating power. Leadership that does not know how many people use which tools can neither choose the right offer nor negotiate its terms.

Which signals show that undeclared use has taken hold?

Without monitoring anyone, a few clues are telling:
subscriptions to AI tools in expense claims or on company cards;
browser extensions or apps installed without going through IT;
documents whose style becomes the same from one department to the next;
sudden gains in speed that nobody explains.

None of these signals calls for a sanction. Each one reveals a need the company has not yet covered.

Which functions are most exposed?

All of them, but not with the same data:
sales, with proposals, price lists, prospect names and the history of exchanges;
human resources, with CVs, appraisals, health or pay information;
finance, with forecasts, margins and unpublished results;
leadership and communications, with reorganisation plans and committee minutes.

A policy becomes clear when it gives, for each function, an example of what can be entrusted to a tool and what never should be.

What does the legal framework say?

Since 2 February 2025, the AI Act, the European AI regulation, has required companies that use AI systems to make sure their people have a sufficient level of AI literacy. Training and informing are no longer just good practice.

The GDPR still applies to any personal data entered into a tool, approved or not. The company remains responsible for what it does with the information of its clients and employees.

The French data protection authority, the CNIL, regularly publishes recommendations on generative AI. They are a solid basis for writing a proportionate policy, without jargon.

How do you choose a business offer rather than a personal account?

Most major vendors offer versions for companies. Their value lies less in the features than in the contractual commitments.

Three questions to ask before signing:
is the data entered used to train the vendor’s models?
where is it hosted, and how long is it kept?
can the company manage the accounts, and close them when an employee leaves?

A clear answer to these three questions is worth more than a long list of features.

Which tools should head the approved list?

The ones that cover the three most frequent needs:
writing or rewording a message;
summarising a long document;
preparing a meeting or a piece of research.

To start, one approved tool per need is enough. More specialised requests, such as data analysis or contract translation, are then handled case by case, with the named contact person.

Do you need a supervised testing space?

Often, yes. A “sandbox” is a space where teams test new tools with fictional or anonymised data, for a limited period.

It answers employees’ legitimate curiosity without exposing the company. And every trial becomes information for leadership: what works, what does not, what deserves approval.

What about contractors and freelancers?

Shadow AI does not stop at your walls. A consultant, an agency or a translator may hand your documents to an AI tool without telling you.

One question before the assignment clears it up: which AI tools will you use, and with which of our information?
A contract clause then sets the rule, just as it already does for confidentiality.

Should employee representatives be involved?

In France, in companies with at least fifty employees, the introduction of new technologies is one of the topics on which the works council is consulted. Rolling out AI tools often falls within that scope.

Beyond the obligation, involving them early has a real advantage: they know the worries on the ground, and they can help you write a policy the teams will accept.

How do you talk about it without worrying teams?

Start with what the company will offer, not with what it will ban. An announcement that opens with “here are the tools you can use from tomorrow” gets heard. One that opens with a list of bans gets worked around.

Then give a single address for questions, with a response time. Trust is earned when a question asked on Monday gets an answer within the week.

What do you do when someone reports a mistake?

Thank them. An employee who reports pasting sensitive information into a tool, or passing on a wrong answer, is doing the company a favour: they make it possible to fix things before the problem grows.

Keep a simple log of these reports, with no names, noting what happened and what changed. After a few months, it becomes the best source for improving the policy. A company that punishes reports stops receiving them, and finds out about its incidents too late.

How do you measure progress?

Four indicators are enough to track the situation from one quarter to the next:
the share of uses declared in the latest review;
the number of people using the approved tools;
the number of questions asked to the contact person;
the average time taken to answer them.

If questions go up, that is a good sign: the rule is known, and teams would rather ask than work around it.

In an SME of twenty people?

The subject looks like something for large groups. Yet it comes up in exactly the same way in a small company, with one advantage: everyone knows each other.

One team meeting is enough to go through the uses. The leader can be the contact person, and the policy fits on one page. What takes months in a group is settled in a few weeks in an SME, as long as you get on with it.

If a client asks you about it?

Some clients are starting to ask their suppliers how they govern AI, especially in security questionnaires and tenders. The question is not about technology. It is about control.

A company that has mapped its uses, approved its tools and written its policy answers in three sentences. One that discovers the question on the day it is asked improvises, and the client can tell.

So all this work also serves a commercial goal: being able to show, with documents to back it up, that you use AI with method. In a market where trust is increasingly on the table, few competitors can present that yet.

A 90-day plan

Three stages, one month each:
from day 1 to day 30, map real uses, starting with the executive committee;
from day 31 to day 60, approve two or three tools that cover the most frequent needs, and write a two-page policy;
from day 61 to day 90, train teams on their own tasks, then measure with the four indicators.

At the end of these three months, the company does not know everything. But it knows what is happening, and it has taken back control.

What shadow AI really reveals

A hidden use is first of all a message: your teams found a gain the company was not offering them. Treating it as a fault means ignoring that message.

Leadership that listens turns a diffuse risk into a roadmap. It knows which needs to cover, which tools to choose and which data to protect first. That is when the AI policy becomes a leadership choice, and no longer a text everyone works around.
