An AI policy that its own
leaders do not follow
is no longer a policy.

September 25, 2026

Short version →

A WalkMe study from July 2025 finds that 78% of employees use AI tools their company has never approved.

A 2025 Cybernews survey adds that 93% of the executives and senior managers surveyed do the same.

The issue is not team discipline but the governance around it.

Three reflexes: acknowledge real usage before banning it, offer safe and simple tools, and start by examining the leadership team’s own practices.

An AI policy is drafted in the executive committee. In the lift afterwards, everyone goes back to their personal AI tool.

A WalkMe study run in July 2025 confirms it at scale: 78% of employees use AI tools their company has never approved. Even more surprising, a 2025 Cybernews survey shows that 93% of the executives and senior managers surveyed do exactly the same.

On the organisational side, Gartner reaches a similar conclusion : 69% of organisations suspect or have evidence that their teams use unauthorised generative AI.

🔍 Why is shadow AI not a question of discipline?

Because usage is massive, and shared all the way to the top of the organisation. When nearly everyone surveyed works around the rule, this is no longer a problem of individuals: it is a sign that the rule does not meet the need.

The issue is not team discipline.
It is the governance around it.

An AI policy that its own leaders do not follow is no longer a policy. It is a shared fiction.

What does shadow AI actually hide?

Behind the expression, there are people trying to save time: summarising a document, rewording a message, preparing a meeting. Nothing malicious, but all of it happens with tools the company has not assessed, and sometimes with information that should not leave the organisation.

The risk is therefore not the tool itself. It is the absence of a clear rule on what can be entrusted to it, and the absence of visibility for management.

Which data should never leave the company?

Customer information, contracts, personal data, non-public financials and anything covered by business secrecy. A useful policy says so in one simple sentence, with examples everyone recognises from their own work.

On the regulatory side, notably GDPR, an SME that uses AI will find its obligations set out in the AI Act and GDPR guide for an SME.

How do you map usage without hunting for culprits?
By asking in a way that does not punish: which tools save you time, and for which tasks? A small-group conversation or a short anonymous questionnaire gives a more honest picture than a control exercise.

The goal is to bring real needs to the surface, then answer them with approved tools.

What are the concrete risks of shadow AI?

Three families of risk.
A leak of information, when a confidential document is pasted into an uncontrolled tool.
A decision based on a wrong, unchecked answer that then circulates as fact.
A compliance risk, when personal data is processed without a framework.

None of these risks requires malicious intent: they arise from an ordinary gesture, made to save time.

Why do bans so often fail?
Because they leave the need intact.
A person banned from a tool that helps them keeps looking for the same gain elsewhere, out of sight. A ban moves usage into the shadows instead of removing it.

A rule that offers a simple, safe alternative has a far better chance of being followed than one that merely forbids.

Why acknowledge real usage before banning it?

🎯 Which reflexes change the game?
Three reflexes are enough to start:
acknowledge real usage,
offer safe and simple tools,
set the example at the top.

Because it already exists, whether we like it or not. Banning a use you do not know about pushes it into the shadows. Mapping it, without hunting for culprits, finally gives an accurate picture of what teams actually do.

How do you offer safe tools that are simpler than the ones people hide?
By providing approved tools that are as quick to pick up as personal ones. If the safe option is heavier than the hidden one, the hidden one wins.

Why start with the leadership team’s own practices?

Because consistency starts at the top. An executive committee that looks honestly at its own usage gives the policy a credibility that no internal memo can replace.

An organisation never secures what it refuses to look at.

What should a useful AI policy contain?
Five elements are often enough:
authorised uses,
forbidden data,
approved tools,
a named person to ask,
a review date.

A two-page policy that everyone has read is worth more than a twenty-page document nobody opens.
It should also be written for those who use it, with their tasks and their words.

How often should it be reviewed?

Regularly, because tools and uses change fast. A quarterly rhythm, with a short item on the executive committee agenda, lets you add an approved tool, remove a use that became risky and check that the rule still matches practice.

A rule frozen for a year describes a situation that no longer exists.

How does a leader set the example?
By applying the policy personally, saying which tools they use and for what, and accepting that the question is put to them.
When executives do what they ask others to do, the rule stops being a constraint and becomes a shared norm.

That is the condition for the policy to stop being a fiction and become a steering tool again.

Where do you start this week?

With a half-hour conversation in the executive committee: which AI tools does each person really use, and with what information? That frank, judgement-free conversation is worth more than a new version of the policy.

It provides the only solid basis for deciding what to allow, what to offer and what to forbid.

How do you choose which tools to approve?
By starting from the real uses found in the organisation, not from a feature catalogue. Three questions help:
– what happens to the information entered,
– who has access to it, and
– can the tool be picked up in a few minutes?

It is better to approve a few well-explained tools than to draw up a long list nobody consults.

What role for the legal function or the delegated executive?

A supporting role, not a blocking one. The named contact answers questions quickly, keeps the list of approved tools up to date and alerts the executive committee when a new use appears. Without a fast answer, teams do without the opinion.

In a small organisation, this contact can be the leader personally, provided their availability is known to everyone.

How do you raise awareness without blaming anyone?
By talking about plausible cases rather than faults. A simple example, such as confidential meeting minutes pasted into a consumer tool, is enough to convey the stakes without singling anyone out.

Awareness works better when it comes from the executive committee itself, which acknowledges its own uses before asking others to change theirs.

What should you do about a use that is already in place?

Map it first, without judgement, then decide: keep it if it is safe, replace it with an approved tool if it is not, or frame it with a clear rule.
Plan a transition period so teams do not lose what saves them time.

Announce the change with its reasons. A use withdrawn without explanation almost always reappears elsewhere.

What should you do about information already shared with an unapproved tool?
Assess calmly what may have been shared, without looking for someone to blame. Depending on the nature of the information, notably if it contains personal data, seek legal advice before deciding what to do next.

Note what was learned and build it into the policy: it is the best way to prevent the situation from recurring.

Who should own this topic in the organisation?

A clearly named member of the executive committee, with an operational relay. The subject touches tools, data, human resources and compliance all at once: without an owner, it is handled piecemeal and nobody has the full picture.

This owner does not need to be an AI specialist. They must know how to ask the right questions and arbitrate when two needs collide.

The same principle applies to every future tool. Each time a new AI service appears on the market, the question will come back: is it approved, for which uses, and with which data? A policy that answers this question quickly, in writing and in plain words, does more for safety than a long document read once and forgotten. Keeping it short, current and visible is the real discipline, and it is a task that belongs to the leadership team, not only to the people who manage the tools.

What shadow AI costs, beyond the risks

Data leaks and compliance usually take up the whole discussion. A quieter cost goes unnoticed: the know-how being built outside the company.

An employee who uses a personal tool every day accumulates effective prompts, message templates and working methods. All of it lives in a private account. The day that person leaves, this capital leaves too, without anyone knowing it existed.

Then come scattered expenses: individual subscriptions paid with a personal card or slipped into expense claims, often several for the same need. One by one, they look harmless. Added up, they fund an AI policy that nobody decided.

Finally, the company loses negotiating power. Leadership that does not know how many people use which tools can neither choose a suitable offer nor discuss its terms.

Which signals show that undeclared use has taken hold?

Without monitoring anyone, a few clues are telling:
subscriptions to AI tools in expense claims or on company cards;
browser extensions or applications installed without going through IT;
documents whose style becomes uniform from one department to another;
sudden gains in speed that nobody explains.

None of these signals calls for a sanction. Each one reveals a need the organisation has not yet met.

Which functions are most exposed?

All of them, but not with the same data:
sales, with proposals, price lists, prospect names and the history of exchanges;
human resources, with CVs, appraisals, health or pay information;
finance, with forecasts, margins and unpublished results;
leadership and communications, with reorganisation plans and committee minutes.

A charter becomes clearer when it gives, for each function, an example of what can be entrusted to a tool and what never should be.

What does the legal framework say?

Since 2 February 2025, the European AI regulation, the AI Act, has required companies that deploy AI systems to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among the people who use them. Training and informing are therefore no longer just good practice.

The GDPR still applies to any personal data entered into a tool, approved or not. The company remains responsible for what happens to the information of its clients and employees.

The French data protection authority, the CNIL, regularly publishes recommendations on the use of generative AI. They provide a solid basis for drafting a proportionate charter, free of jargon.

How do you choose a business offer rather than a personal account?

Most major vendors offer versions designed for companies. Their value lies less in features than in contractual commitments.

Three questions to ask before signing:
is the data entered used to train the vendor’s models?
where is it hosted, and how long is it kept?
can the company administer the accounts, and close them when an employee leaves?

A clear answer to these three questions is worth more than a long list of features.

Which tools should head the approved list?

Those that meet the three most frequent needs:
drafting or rewording a message;
summarising a long document;
preparing a meeting or a piece of research.

One approved tool per need is enough to start. More specific requests, such as data analysis or contract translation, are then handled case by case with the designated contact.

Do you need a supervised testing space?

Often, yes. A “sandbox” is a space where teams test new tools with fictitious or anonymised data, for a limited period.

It meets employees’ legitimate curiosity without exposing the company. It also turns each trial into information for leadership: what works, what does not, and what deserves approval.

What should you plan with contractors and freelancers?

Shadow AI does not stop at the company’s walls. A consultant, an agency or a translator may feed your documents into an AI tool without telling you.

A question asked before the assignment clarifies the situation: which AI tools will you use, and with which of our information? A clause in the contract then sets the rule, just as is already done for confidentiality.

Should employee representatives be involved?

In France, in companies with at least fifty employees, the introduction of new technologies is one of the topics on which the social and economic committee is consulted. Rolling out AI tools often falls within that scope.

Beyond the obligation, involving them early has an advantage: they know the concerns on the ground, and can help shape a charter that teams will accept.

How do you talk about it with teams without worrying them?

By starting with what the company will offer, not with what it will ban. An announcement that opens with “here are the tools you can use from tomorrow” is heard. An announcement that opens with a list of prohibitions is worked around.

Then give a single address for questions, with a response time. Trust is earned when a question asked on Monday gets an answer within the week.

What should you do when someone reports a mistake?

Thank them. An employee who reports having pasted sensitive information into a tool, or passed on a wrong answer, does the company a favour: it can correct course before the problem grows.

Keep a simple log of these reports, without names, recording what happened and what was changed. After a few months, it becomes the best source for improving the charter. An organisation that punishes reports stops receiving them, and discovers its incidents too late.

How do you measure progress?

Four indicators are enough to track the situation from one quarter to the next:
the share of uses declared in the latest review;
the number of people using the approved tools;
the number of questions asked to the designated contact;
the average response time to those questions.

If the number of questions rises, that is a good sign: the rule is known, and teams prefer to ask rather than work around it.

And in a twenty-person SME?

The topic seems reserved for large groups. Yet it arises in the same way in a small business, with one advantage: everyone knows each other.

One team meeting is enough to review the uses. The owner can act as the designated contact, and the charter fits on one page. What takes months in a group can be settled in a few weeks in an SME, provided someone gets started.

What if a client asks you about it?

Some clients are starting to ask their suppliers how they govern the use of AI, notably in security questionnaires and tenders. The question is not about technology: it is about control.

A company that has reviewed its uses, approved its tools and written its charter answers in three sentences. One that discovers the question on the day it is asked improvises, and the client notices.

The work described in this article therefore also serves a commercial goal: being able to show, with documents to back it up, that AI is used with method. In a market where trust is increasingly debated, few competitors can yet present that argument.

A 90-day plan

Three steps, one month each:
from day 1 to day 30, map actual uses, starting with the executive committee;
from day 31 to day 60, approve two or three tools covering the most frequent needs, and write a two-page charter;
from day 61 to day 90, train teams on their own tasks, then measure with the four indicators.

After these three months, the company does not know everything. But it knows what is happening, and it has taken back control.

What shadow AI really reveals

A hidden use is first of all a message: teams have found a gain the organisation was not offering them. Treating it as misconduct means ignoring that message.

Leadership that listens turns a diffuse risk into a roadmap. It knows which needs to meet, which tools to choose and which data to protect first. That is the moment the AI policy becomes a leadership choice, rather than a text everyone works around.
