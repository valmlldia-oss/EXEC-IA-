More AI tools do not mean more impact. It is not a tools problem. It is a trade-off problem.

Expanded version of a LinkedIn post · September 9, 2026

Short version →

More AI tools do not mean more impact: it is a trade-off problem, not a tools problem. Three criteria help sort them: a real challenge, a named budget and owner, an accepted reversibility. Without that sorting, a team ends up running five initiatives halfway instead of two all the way through — a Gartner study predicted that 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned for lack of clear business value. The ritual that prevents this drift: a short quarterly review where every active tool has to justify its place, instead of waiting for one big annual clean-up.

If you lead a communications team, you know the reflex: as soon as a new AI tool comes out, you try it. Then another. Then another.

Coordinating communications for the McKinsey France Alumni network, I saw how easy it is to add one more tool, and how rarely anyone stops to ask which one really matters.

That reflex is not carelessness. Every new tool promises to solve what the last one did not, and skipping it feels like falling behind — a real pressure, kept alive by how fast the market ships new versions.

It is not a tools problem. It is a trade-off problem.

A Gartner study predicted that 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept, for lack of clear business value. That number does not mean generative AI fails. It means a tool with no owner, no budget and no way to be judged rarely survives contact with a real organisation.

The easiest symptom to spot: a team that can rattle off the last five tools it tried, but struggles to name one that changed something measurable. That is not a lack of curiosity — it is a missing filter between trying and adopting.

🛠️ Why do more AI tools not mean more impact?

Because it means more dispersion, and a team running five initiatives halfway instead of two all the way through.

🎯 What three criteria decide which AI tool is worth keeping?

  1. 1. A real brand, crisis or launch challenge, not a trend to follow?
  2. 2. A committed budget and a named owner ready to decide, not just to watch?
  3. 3. A tool you could switch off tomorrow morning without anyone missing it?

Why these three criteria specifically?

A brand, crisis or launch challenge forces a use to prove itself against something real, not a trend. A named budget and owner turn an experiment into a decision someone answers for. And a tool you could switch off tomorrow without anyone noticing was never carrying real weight to begin with — keeping it running out of habit is exactly how initiatives pile up without ever being finished.

Who should carry this trade-off role on a small team?

On a small communications team, this role does not need a dedicated position. It is enough that one person, often whoever already owns the budget or the calendar, holds the explicit mandate to apply the three criteria to any new tool before it settles in for good. That mandate changes little day to day, except at one precise moment: when someone proposes trying a new tool. At that point, one question is enough: what real challenge does this new attempt serve that the tools already in place do not cover?

Without that question asked systematically, every tool settles in by default, never by decision. The risk is not that one bad tool takes hold: it is that ten mediocre ones coexist, each covering a need another already half-covers.

What separates a tool you keep from one you tolerate

A tool you keep has a clear answer to all three criteria: a named challenge, a committed budget, someone ready to answer for it in front of the team. A tool you tolerate usually has none of the three: it was simply tried one day, no one made the call to stop it, and it keeps running in the background, consuming team time no one consciously chose to spend.

The difference does not show in the tool itself. It shows in the team’s ability to answer, without hesitating, the question: why this one rather than another? When the answer takes too long, that is the sign no trade-off ever happened — the tool stayed out of habit, not choice.

What happens when no one decides?

In practice, this sorting runs into a simple obstacle: no one on the team explicitly owns it. Everyone keeps the tool they personally tested, because they invested time in it and do not want to see it dropped. Without someone whose role is precisely to arbitrate between initiatives, with no attachment to any of them, the list only grows. That gap is never announced as such: no one decides not to sort, the list simply keeps growing while everyone waits for someone else to take it on.

Cutting between tools also means accepting that a dropped tool is not a failed project, but proof the selection criteria worked. A team that never drops anything has probably never really sorted anything.

How do you apply this sorting day to day?

In practice, this sorting works better as a ritual than as a one-off decision: a short quarterly review where every active tool has to justify its place against the three criteria. Whatever no longer passes gets cut, without a drawn-out debate. That format avoids the most common trap: waiting for one big annual clean-up, by which point the list has already doubled. A short, repeated ritual costs less energy than one big annual clean-up — and more importantly, it keeps the list from ever getting long enough to need one.

How do you introduce this ritual without adding a new burden?

This ritual works better when it stays short: fifteen minutes are enough if every tool is judged against the same three criteria, always in the same order. What makes it heavier is not its frequency, it is the temptation to add new criteria at every pass. Three stable criteria, applied without exception, beat ten criteria adjusted case by case.

The sign that this ritual works is not the number of tools it eliminates: it is that the list of active tools stops growing on its own between reviews.
