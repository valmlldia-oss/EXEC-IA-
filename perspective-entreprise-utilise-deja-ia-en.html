Adopting AI is one thing.
Creating value from it is another.

Expanded version of a LinkedIn post · September 8, 2026

Short version →

Adopting AI is one thing; creating value from it is another. Individual productivity gains are already widespread, but few companies can show a measurable effect on their bottom line. Three markers help decide which uses deserve real investment: frequency, measurability, and independence from any one expert. The Klarna case, forced to rehire human agents after replacing its customer service with AI, shows what happens when an individual gain gets mistaken for a real outcome. The real question is not adopting AI faster, but knowing which of its uses deserves to be scaled.

The latest State of AI report from McKinsey & Company points to a telling contrast: individual productivity gains are already widely seen, while the contribution to the company’s financial results remains more limited.

That gap raises an essential question, it seems to me.

What if the next challenge for AI were no longer just adoption, but choosing between its uses?

  • Which uses truly deserve investment?
  • Which ones should come first?
  • And which should you have the discipline to drop?

🎯 Why does the ability to choose become strategic as technological possibilities multiply?

In practice, this trade-off rests on three simple markers. First, frequency: a use repeated every week across several teams carries more weight than an impressive one-off demo, however striking. Second, measurability: if nobody can say, six months from now, what a given use actually changed, it was never tied to a result the leadership team tracks. Third, dependency: a use that only works because one expert carries it will not survive that person moving to a new role; one that holds without them has a real chance of lasting. All three markers apply before the investment, never after the fact, that is what separates a chosen use from one simply left to take root.

What does this look like in practice?

Take a generic example: a sales team uses AI to pre-qualify leads before the first call. High frequency, a measurable outcome, the conversion rate of that first meeting, and the tool keeps working even if the person who set it up moves on. Compare that with an internal chatbot deployed to "show technological progress," with no regular use and no tracked metric: the first use passes all three filters, the second passes none. The test fits in one line: if this use vanished tomorrow, would anyone notice on a result leadership already tracks?

How do you put this trade-off in place without adding a new layer?

In a mid-sized company, this trade-off role does not need a standing committee or a dedicated position. It is often enough that one person, already identified, holds the explicit mandate to review every AI use case that is a candidate for real investment against the three markers. That person does not need technical expertise: they need access to both worlds, the field using the tool and the metrics leadership already tracks.

That mandate fits in one sentence, not a governance charter: who, at your company, currently has the authority to say no to an AI use that fails the three markers, even if the team that adopted it likes it? As long as the answer stays unclear, every use keeps running by default, never by decision.

What this means for a mid-sized company’s AI budget

This has a direct budget consequence. Part of a mid-sized company’s AI budget should be set aside not for new uses, but for checking the ones already deployed: how many are actually tracked by a metric leadership reviews every month? That check costs little, usually a few hours, and often saves from investing further in a use that never moved past individual gain.

It also has a useful side effect: it surfaces the uses that, conversely, would deserve support they never received, simply because no one had the mandate to bring them to leadership’s attention.

This is the question I want to explore further here: how do you move from what AI makes possible to the decisions that genuinely create value?

How do you tell an individual gain from an outcome?

A simple way to sort uses: separate what lightens a task from what changes an outcome. A meeting note summarised faster is an individual gain. A shorter decision cycle, a lead better qualified before the first call, a costly error caught in time, those are outcomes. The first kind of gain shows up immediately and fades just as fast. The second has to be sought, measured and chosen upfront. It is never a by-product of chance.

The same McKinsey report points to a telling shift in the opposite direction: the share of large companies deploying AI agents across the whole organisation, rather than within a single department, rose from 27% to 40% in one year. These are the companies starting to move beyond pure individual gain, precisely because they chose where to concentrate effort instead of letting every team experiment on its own.

For an executive committee, the question is therefore not "how do we adopt AI faster" but "which use, once chosen and backed, will change a result we already track." That question comes before the tool, never after.

In practice, that trade-off rarely belongs to one person. The employee who found a useful case has neither the overview nor the mandate to decide where to extend it. The executive committee has the mandate, but rarely the time to test every use surfacing from the field. Between the two, a role is usually missing: someone who knows the field well enough to spot what deserves to be scaled, and knows the leadership priorities well enough to tie it to a result they already track.

Who should own this trade-off?

That trade-off role, between what surfaces from the field and what leadership prioritises, is exactly what most organisations lack: not a shortage of tools, nor a shortage of goodwill, but the absence of someone whose explicit mandate is to decide.

Where should you start?

In practice, it starts with a simple inventory: list the AI uses already active in the organisation, usually more numerous than expected, then run each through the three markers. Most leadership teams discover at this stage that they are already funding, unknowingly, several uses that would pass none of the three tests, and that one or two others would deserve real backing they never received. That inventory rarely takes more than one meeting; the real work starts after, when it means agreeing to stop a familiar use because it fails the test, even before anyone has complained about it.

What does a real case reveal about confusing individual gain with outcome?

The case of Klarna, the Swedish buy-now-pay-later company, illustrates this confusion well. In February 2024, its chief executive announced that its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time customer service agents, resolving requests faster. A year later, the company announced it was rehiring human agents, publicly explaining it had not managed to keep service quality up with AI alone.

The first figure measured an individual gain: fewer calls, faster resolution. It did not measure the outcome the company actually tracks: customer satisfaction and long-term retention. That is exactly the distinction this piece makes: a gain that shows up immediately is not yet an outcome, until it has been checked against the metric leadership already tracks.
