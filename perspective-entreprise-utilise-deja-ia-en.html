Adopting AI is one thing.
Creating value from it
is another.

Article developed from my LinkedIn post (8 September 2026)
Updated on 30 September 2026

Short version →

SME leaders: adopting AI is one thing; creating value from it is another.

80% of companies see their teams save time with AI. Only 37% see the effect on their results.

To pick the uses that deserve real investment, three questions are enough: does it come up often? Can it be measured? Does it hold without one expert?

Klarna had to rehire humans after handing its customer service to AI. It had mistaken a time saving for a result.

The question to ask yourself: of all your AI uses, which one deserves to be rolled out across the whole company?

You run an SME and your teams already use AI? This figure from the latest State of AI report by McKinsey & Company is for you. Across nearly 1,700 organisations in 97 countries, 80% see their teams save time thanks to AI. Yet only 37% see the effect on their operating results.

Everything that remains to be done sits between those two figures.
What strikes me is that AI enters the company through employees, not through leadership. Everyone finds their own shortcut. Summarising meeting notes. Roughing out a first draft. Searching faster. These gains are real, and nobody disputes them. But they stay scattered, and none of them reaches the bottom line on its own.

What if the next challenge for AI were no longer just adoption, but choosing between its uses?

Which uses deserve investment?
Which ones should come first?
And which should you have the discipline to drop?

🎯 Why does the ability to choose become strategic as technological possibilities multiply?

Today, with AI, you can try everything. That is exactly the trap: when you test everything, nobody decides anything.
To choose, I suggest three very simple questions.
One: does the use come up often? A tool used every week by several teams weighs more than a spectacular demo done once.
Two: can it be measured? If nobody can say, six months from now, what it changed, it was never tied to a figure leadership tracks.
Three: does it hold without an expert? A use that only works thanks to one person will stop the day that person changes jobs.
These three questions come before you invest. Once the money is spent, saying no becomes much harder.

What does this look like in practice?

Picture a sales team using AI to prepare its prospects before the first call.
They use it every day. The result can be measured: the conversion rate of that first meeting. The tool keeps running even if the person who set it up leaves. Three questions, three yeses.
Now picture an internal chatbot launched to “show the company is ahead of the curve”. Hardly anyone uses it, and no figure is tracked. Three questions, three nos.
Want an even quicker test? Ask yourself: if this use vanished tomorrow, would anyone notice it in a figure leadership already looks at?

How do you put this trade-off in place without adding a new layer?

In an SME, you do not need a committee or a new job for this. One person is enough, often someone already on the team. You give them a clear mission: run every AI project through the three questions before any money goes in.
They do not need to be technical. They need to know both sides: the people on the ground who use the tools, and the figures leadership follows. It is often the role a leader’s right hand already plays, without the title.

One question to see where you stand: at your company, who can say no to an AI tool that fails the three questions, even if the team loves it? If no name comes to mind, your uses are settling in on their own. Nobody chose them.

What does this change for a mid-sized company’s AI budget?

It changes how you split the budget. Before buying a new tool, keep a small share of the AI budget to check the ones already running. One question is enough: how many are tracked by a figure leadership looks at every month?
That check costs little, a few hours as a rule. It often stops you from putting more money into a tool that never went beyond one person’s comfort.

It has another benefit. It brings out the uses that, on the contrary, deserve a push: the ones nobody thought of raising with leadership.

That is the question I want to dig into with you: how do you get from everything AI makes possible to the decisions that create value?

How do you tell an individual gain from an outcome?

I sort things into two columns. On one side, what makes a task lighter. On the other, what changes a result.
Meeting notes summarised faster? That is a gain for one person.
A decision made sooner, a client better prepared before the first call, an error caught before it costs money? Now we are talking about results.
The first gain shows up straight away, but nobody remembers it three months later.
The second has to be sought out, measured and chosen from the start. It never happens by chance.

The same McKinsey report shows an interesting shift. The share of large companies deploying AI agents across the whole organisation, and no longer in a single department, rose from 27% to 40% in one year.
To me, these are the companies starting to move beyond individual gains. They chose where to put their effort, instead of letting every team test things in its own corner.

For an executive committee, the question becomes: “which use, once chosen and backed, will change a figure we already track?”
It comes before choosing the tool.

In real life, nobody is well placed to decide alone. The employee who found the clever shortcut has neither the overview nor the authority to extend it.
Leadership has the authority. But it rarely has the time to test everything coming up from the field.
In between, someone is often missing. Someone who knows the field well enough to spot what is worth it, and knows leadership well enough to tie it to a figure it tracks.

Who should own this trade-off?

Someone whose mission it clearly is. In most companies, the tools are there. So is the goodwill. What is missing is a person in charge of deciding between what comes up from the field and leadership’s priorities.
In a large company, that may be a head of transformation. In an SME, it is often the leader, their right hand or the finance director. The title does not matter. What matters is that everyone knows who decides, and that this person is allowed to say no.
Without that name, every team keeps its own tools, subscriptions pile up, and nobody knows which ones are worth anything.

Where should you start?

With a very simple inventory. List the AI uses already in place at your company. There are almost always more than you think. Then run them through the three questions.
Most leadership teams then discover they are already paying, without knowing it, for tools that pass none of the tests, while one or two others deserve backing.
The inventory often fits into one meeting. The hard part comes after: agreeing to stop a tool everyone got used to, because it fails the test, even if nobody has complained yet.

What does a real case reveal about confusing individual gain with outcome?

The story of Klarna, the Swedish buy-now-pay-later company, says a lot. In February 2024, its chief executive announced that its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time customer service agents, and answering faster.
A year later, the company was rehiring human agents. It explained publicly that with AI alone, it could not keep service quality up.

The first figure measured a gain: fewer calls, faster answers. It said nothing about what the company tracks day to day: how satisfied and loyal its customers stay over time.
That is the whole point. A gain that shows up straight away only becomes a result once it has been checked against the figure leadership already tracks.

💬 And in your organisation,

is the challenge still adopting AI…
or now knowing where it deserves to be deployed?

Four more questions to take the thinking further.

Three families of uses, three levels of value

Not all AI uses are equal. Lump them together and you end up comparing things that cannot be compared.

I sort them into three families:
comfort uses, which lighten one person’s workload: writing, summarising, translating;
process uses, which change the way several people work together: handling requests, preparing quotes, chasing follow-ups;
decision uses, which change what leadership knows at the moment of choosing: analysing margins, spotting at-risk customers, forecasting workload.

The first family spreads on its own. The other two only move forward if someone carries them, because they touch the organisation itself.

How do you put a value on a use, simply?

A back-of-the-envelope calculation is enough to start: the time saved each time, multiplied by the number of times in the month, multiplied by the hourly cost of the people involved.

Against that, put everything the use costs. The subscription, of course, but not only.

Four costs often slip through the cracks:
review time, essential as long as the tool can get things wrong;
training time, at the start and then with every new hire;
upkeep of the instructions given to the tool, which age along with your products and your rules;
coordination time, when the use moves from one team to another.

A tool that saves you ten minutes but needs eight to check saves you nothing. It just moves the work around.

How do you avoid the pilot that never ends?

Many companies pile up trials that never turn into decisions. The test drags on, the team gets used to it, and nobody decides.

The fix fits in one rule: set the exit before the entry. Before launching a trial, write down how long it lasts, the figure you will track and the threshold at which you roll it out or stop.

A pilot with no end date is no longer a pilot. It is a use installed by default, one nobody will ever have to evaluate.

What role for the finance function?

A central one, and often forgotten. Finance knows the real costs, can read a gain, and already runs the meetings where spending is decided.

Bring them in early. The conversation changes: you stop talking about a tool people like, and start talking about the return on an investment, with the same standards as any other project.

In an SME without a finance director, the accountant or the leader can play that role. On one condition: looking at AI uses with the same rigour as every other expense.

Why does data quality so often decide the result?

An AI tool is only as good as what you give it. An incomplete customer file, prices scattered across several documents, procedures nobody ever wrote down: even the most powerful tool will not get much out of that.

Before looking for the best tool, ask yourself a simple question: does the information this use needs exist, is it up to date, and is someone responsible for it?

If the answer is no, your first project is putting that information in order. AI will come after.

How do you present a decision to the executive committee?

On a single page, with five headings:
the use, described in one sentence everyone understands;
the figure tracked, with its value before and after;
the full cost, subscription included;
the main risk, and how to keep it under control;
the proposed decision: roll out, adjust or stop.

This format forces clarity. If a use does not fit on one page, it is not ready to be decided yet.

When should you speed up rather than slow down?

Choosing does not mean slowing down. When a use passes the three questions, the right decision is often to move fast, before the momentum fades.

To roll out a use, four steps:
document it, with the instructions that work and the mistakes to avoid;
train new teams on their own cases, not on a generic demo;
equip it, with a business plan suited to the number of users;
measure again three months later, to check the gain holds at a larger scale.

A use that works in one team is good news. It becomes a result for the company the day it works elsewhere, without the person who found it.

Do you need a separate AI budget?

Not necessarily. A separate budget attracts projects looking for money more than projects answering a need.

I prefer another route: each function pays for its uses out of its own budget, with one shared rule. Every expense is tied to a figure the function already tracks. AI then becomes one way among others to reach an existing goal, rather than an expense to justify for its own sake.

A small central reserve can complete the picture, to fund the uses that cut across several teams and belong to nobody.

What about ideas that fit into no box?

Keep a small space for them, limited in time and in resources. Some ideas do not pass the three questions yet simply because they are too new to be measured.

Give them a small share of the effort, with a clear deadline. You do not stifle innovation, and you do not let it take over your priorities. At the deadline, the idea joins the normal decision process, or it stops.

And what about the competition?

The fear of falling behind often pushes companies to multiply trials. I understand that fear. But it is looking at the wrong risk.

Falling behind does not come from the number of tools tested. It comes from being unable to turn good trials into shared practices. A company that tests little but rolls out well moves faster than one that tests everything and decides nothing.

How do you know the process is working?

Three signs to look for after six months:
fewer uses running, but more of them tracked by a figure;
at least one use stopped, and explained to the teams;
at least one use extended beyond the team that found it.

If no use has been stopped, the choosing probably never happened. It was only announced.

What choosing really changes

Choosing means accepting to give things up. Attractive uses, flattering demos, sometimes even the tool the leader likes.

In return, the company gains something rare: the ability to focus its attention on what changes its results. When possibilities multiply every month, that is an advantage in its own right.

How do you link an AI use to a company result?

→

Follow the thread from the task to the decision. Which task does the tool shorten? How much time does it free up? What is that time used for next?
The last question is the one that counts. Without it, the gain stays individual.

A simple test: try saying out loud “thanks to this use, we decide or deliver faster what matters to our customers”. If the sentence does not ring true, the use is not yet linked to a result.

An example. A tool drafts meeting notes and saves a manager one hour a week. If that hour goes into calling back two waiting clients, the gain becomes a result. If it disappears into emails, nobody will ever see it.

Which indicator should you choose to judge a first use?

→

Just one, and one everybody understands: the time saved on a precise task, the number of errors avoided, or the response time to a customer. Track five, and nobody looks at any of them.

Choose it before the test, never after. Otherwise you always end up picking the figure that confirms what you hoped.

Write down the starting point too. Without a figure before the trial, you cannot prove progress afterwards. One week of very simple tracking, even by hand, is enough for that baseline. It is the step most often forgotten, and it is what makes the decision indisputable in committee.

How often should you review your choices?

→

Often, and without making a big deal of it. Once a quarter is a good rhythm. Tools change fast, so do uses, and a decision made a year ago may no longer match what is happening today.

No need for a long committee. A list of uses, what each one brought, and a decision for each: keep, adjust or stop.

The simplest way: hook this review onto a meeting that already exists, such as the quarterly budget review. It then no longer depends on anyone’s goodwill, and it does not get dropped when the diary fills up.

What do you tell teams when a use is stopped?

→

The truth, simply.
A team that understands why a tool is being stopped accepts the decision more easily. It will even suggest another solution more readily.

Stopping a use is part of the job. It even proves the company is choosing, instead of piling up tools.

Remember to thank those who tested it. Their trial taught you something, even if the answer is no. A team that is not penalised for a stopped test will want to try another. That is how a culture of trying takes root, without anyone being afraid of getting it wrong.
