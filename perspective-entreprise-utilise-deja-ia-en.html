Adopting AI is one thing.
Creating value from it
is another.

Expanded version of a LinkedIn post
September 8, 2026 · updated September 25, 2026

Short version →

Adopting AI is one thing; creating value from it is another.

Individual productivity gains are already widespread, but few companies can show a measurable effect on their bottom line.

Three markers help decide which uses deserve real investment: frequency, measurability, and independence from any one expert.

The Klarna case, forced to rehire human agents after replacing its customer service with AI, shows what happens when an individual gain gets mistaken for a real outcome.

The real question is not adopting AI faster, but knowing which of its uses deserves to be scaled.

The latest State of AI report from McKinsey & Company points to a telling contrast: individual productivity gains are already widely seen, while the contribution to the company’s financial results remains more limited.

That gap raises an essential question, it seems to me.

What if the next challenge for AI were no longer just adoption, but choosing between its uses?

Which uses truly deserve investment?
Which ones should come first?
And which should you have the discipline to drop?

🎯 Why does the ability to choose become strategic as technological possibilities multiply?

In practice, this trade-off rests on three simple markers. First, frequency: a use repeated every week across several teams carries more weight than an impressive one-off demo, however striking.
Second, measurability: if nobody can say, six months from now, what a given use actually changed, it was never tied to a result the leadership team tracks.
Third, dependency: a use that only works because one expert carries it will not survive that person moving to a new role; one that holds without them has a real chance of lasting.
All three markers apply before the investment, never after the fact, that is what separates a chosen use from one simply left to take root.

What does this look like in practice?

Take a generic example: a sales team uses AI to pre-qualify leads before the first call.
High frequency, a measurable outcome, the conversion rate of that first meeting, and the tool keeps working even if the person who set it up moves on.
Compare that with an internal chatbot deployed to "show technological progress," with no regular use and no tracked metric: the first use passes all three filters, the second passes none. The test fits in one line: if this use vanished tomorrow, would anyone notice on a result leadership already tracks?

How do you put this trade-off in place without adding a new layer?

In a mid-sized company, this trade-off role does not need a standing committee or a dedicated position. It is often enough that one person, already identified, holds the explicit mandate to review every AI use case that is a candidate for real investment against the three markers.
That person does not need technical expertise: they need access to both worlds, the field using the tool and the metrics leadership already tracks.

That mandate fits in one sentence, not a governance charter: who, at your company, currently has the authority to say no to an AI use that fails the three markers, even if the team that adopted it likes it? As long as the answer stays unclear, every use keeps running by default, never by decision.

What does this change for a mid-sized company’s AI budget?

This has a direct budget consequence. Part of a mid-sized company’s AI budget should be set aside not for new uses, but for checking the ones already deployed: how many are actually tracked by a metric leadership reviews every month?
That check costs little, usually a few hours, and often saves from investing further in a use that never moved past individual gain.

It also has a useful side effect: it surfaces the uses that, conversely, would deserve support they never received, simply because no one had the mandate to bring them to leadership’s attention.

This is the question I want to explore further here: how do you move from what AI makes possible to the decisions that genuinely create value?

How do you tell an individual gain from an outcome?

A simple way to sort uses: separate what lightens a task from what changes an outcome. A meeting note summarised faster is an individual gain.
A shorter decision cycle, a lead better qualified before the first call, a costly error caught in time, those are outcomes.
The first kind of gain shows up immediately and fades just as fast.
The second has to be sought, measured and chosen upfront. It is never a by-product of chance.

The same McKinsey report points to a telling shift in the opposite direction: the share of large companies deploying AI agents across the whole organisation, rather than within a single department, rose from 27% to 40% in one year.
These are the companies starting to move beyond pure individual gain, precisely because they chose where to concentrate effort instead of letting every team experiment on its own.

For an executive committee, the question is therefore not "how do we adopt AI faster" but "which use, once chosen and backed, will change a result we already track."
That question comes before the tool, never after.

In practice, that trade-off rarely belongs to one person. The employee who found a useful case has neither the overview nor the mandate to decide where to extend it.
The executive committee has the mandate, but rarely the time to test every use surfacing from the field.
Between the two, a role is usually missing: someone who knows the field well enough to spot what deserves to be scaled, and knows the leadership priorities well enough to tie it to a result they already track.

Who should own this trade-off?

That trade-off role, between what surfaces from the field and what leadership prioritises, is exactly what most organisations lack: not a shortage of tools, nor a shortage of goodwill, but the absence of someone whose explicit mandate is to decide.

Where should you start?

In practice, it starts with a simple inventory: list the AI uses already active in the organisation, usually more numerous than expected, then run each through the three markers.
Most leadership teams discover at this stage that they are already funding, unknowingly, several uses that would pass none of the three tests, and that one or two others would deserve real backing they never received.
That inventory rarely takes more than one meeting; the real work starts after, when it means agreeing to stop a familiar use because it fails the test, even before anyone has complained about it.

What does a real case reveal about confusing individual gain with outcome?

The case of Klarna, the Swedish buy-now-pay-later company, illustrates this confusion well. In February 2024, its chief executive announced that its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time customer service agents, resolving requests faster.
A year later, the company announced it was rehiring human agents, publicly explaining it had not managed to keep service quality up with AI alone.

The first figure measured an individual gain: fewer calls, faster resolution. It did not measure the outcome the company actually tracks: customer satisfaction and long-term retention. That is exactly the distinction this piece makes: a gain that shows up immediately is not yet an outcome, until it has been checked against the metric leadership already tracks.

Three families of use, three levels of value

Not all uses of AI play in the same league. Telling them apart avoids comparing things that cannot be compared.

They fall into three families:
comfort uses, which lighten an individual task: drafting, summarising, translating;
process uses, which change a workflow shared by several people: handling requests, preparing quotes, following up on reminders;
decision uses, which change what leadership knows at the moment of choosing: margin analysis, spotting at-risk clients, workload forecasts.

The first family spreads on its own. The other two only progress if someone carries them, because they touch the organisation.

How do you put a simple figure on the value of a use?

A back-of-the-envelope calculation is enough to start: time saved per occurrence, multiplied by the number of occurrences in a month, multiplied by the hourly cost of the people involved.

Against that, you must set everything the use consumes, not only the subscription.

Four items are often forgotten:
human review time, essential as long as the tool can make mistakes;
training time, at the start and for every new hire;
upkeep of the instructions given to the tool, which age along with products and rules;
coordination time, when the use crosses several teams.

A use that saves ten minutes but requires eight minutes of checking is not a gain: it is work moved elsewhere.

How do you avoid the pilot that never ends?

Many organisations pile up trials that never become decisions. The test drags on, the team gets used to it, and nobody decides.

The remedy fits in one rule: set the exit before the entry. Before launching a trial, write down its duration, the indicator tracked and the threshold that will trigger roll-out or stop.

A pilot with no end date is not a pilot. It is a use installed by default, with the comfort of never being assessed.

What role for the finance function?

A central one, often underestimated. Finance knows the real costs, can read a gain and already runs the meetings where spending is decided.

Involving it early changes the nature of the discussion: the conversation is no longer about enthusiasm for a tool, but about the return on an investment, held to the same standards as any other project.

In an SME without a finance director, the chartered accountant or the owner can play this role, provided uses are examined with the same rigour as other costs.

Why does data quality often decide the outcome?

An AI tool is only as good as what it is given. An incomplete client file, prices scattered across several documents or procedures never written down limit what the tool can produce, however powerful it is.

Before looking for the most powerful tool, one simple question saves time: does the information this use needs exist, is it up to date, and is someone responsible for it?

If the answer is no, the first project is not AI. It is putting information in order.

How do you present a trade-off to the executive committee?

On a single page, with five headings:
the use, described in one sentence everyone understands;
the indicator tracked, with its value before and after;
the full cost, subscription included;
the main risk, and how it is managed;
the proposed decision: roll out, adjust or stop.

This format forces clarity. A use that does not fit on one page is not yet ready to be decided on.

When should you speed up rather than slow down?

Deciding does not mean slowing down. When a use passes the three markers, the right decision is often to move fast, before momentum fades.

Rolling out a use takes four steps:
document it, with the instructions that work and the mistakes to avoid;
train new teams on their own cases, not on a general demonstration;
equip it, choosing a business offer suited to the number of users;
measure again three months later, to check that the gain holds at scale.

A use that succeeds in one team is not yet a result for the company. It becomes one when it succeeds elsewhere, without its inventor.

Do you need a separate AI budget?

Not necessarily. A standalone budget attracts projects looking for funding rather than those that meet a need.

Another route is to let each function fund its uses from its own budget, with a common rule: every expense is tied to an indicator the function already tracks. AI then becomes one means among others to reach an existing goal, not a cost line to be justified for its own sake.

A small central reserve can complement this, to fund uses that cross several teams and belong to nobody.

What about ideas that fit no box?

Give them a limited space, in time as well as in resources. Some ideas do not yet pass the three markers because they are too recent to be measured.

Granting these explorations a modest share of effort, with a clear deadline, avoids stifling innovation without letting it overrun priorities. At the deadline, the idea joins the normal decision circuit, or stops.

And what about the competition?

The fear of falling behind often drives companies to multiply trials. It is understandable, but it misjudges the real risk.

Falling behind does not come from the number of tools tested. It comes from being unable to turn good trials into shared practice. A company that tests little but rolls out well moves faster than one that tests everything and decides nothing.

How do you know the trade-off is working?

Three signs, to check after six months:
fewer uses in progress, but more of them tracked by an indicator;
at least one use stopped, and explained to teams;
at least one use rolled out beyond the team that found it.

If no use has been stopped, the trade-off probably never happened: it was only announced.

What deciding really changes

Deciding means accepting to give things up. Giving up appealing uses, flattering demonstrations, sometimes the very tool the leader likes.

In return, the organisation gains something rare: the ability to focus its attention on what truly changes its results. In a field where possibilities multiply every month, that ability becomes an advantage in its own right.

How do you link an AI use to a company result?

→

Follow the thread from the task to the decision: which task does the tool shorten, how much time does that free up, and what is that time actually used for? Without this last step, the gain remains individual.

A simple sentence serves as a test: “thanks to this use, we decide or deliver faster what matters to our customers”. If it cannot be said, the use is not yet linked to a result.

For example, a tool that drafts meeting notes saves a manager one hour a week. If that hour goes into calling back two waiting clients, the gain becomes a result; if it dissolves into emails, it stays invisible.

Which indicator should you choose to judge a first use?

→

Just one, understood by everyone: the time saved on a precise task, the number of errors avoided or the response time to a customer. Multiplying indicators dilutes attention and makes the decision harder.

The indicator must exist before the test, not after. Otherwise it is chosen to confirm what was already hoped.

Measure the starting point too: without a figure before the trial, there is no way to prove progress. One week of simple tracking, noted by hand if need be, is enough to set that baseline.

How often should you review your choices?

→

At a regular, short rhythm, for example every quarter. Tools, uses and needs change fast, and a choice made a year ago no longer necessarily describes today’s reality.

This review does not need a long committee: a list of uses, what each brought and one decision for each, keep, adjust or stop.

Attach this review to a meeting that already exists, such as the quarterly budget review: it then no longer depends on everyone’s goodwill.

What do you tell teams when a use is stopped?

→

The reason, plainly. A team that understands why a tool disappears accepts the decision better and is better placed to suggest an alternative.

Stopping a use is also part of the choice: it proves the organisation selects instead of accumulating.

Thank those who tested it as well: their trial produced useful information, even if the answer is no. A team that is not penalised for a stopped test will agree to try another.
