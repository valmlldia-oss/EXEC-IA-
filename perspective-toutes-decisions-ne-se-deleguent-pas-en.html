Not every decision can be delegated. Not even to an AI.

Expanded version of a LinkedIn post · September 8, 2026

Short version →

Not every decision can be delegated to an AI, however capable. A simple test helps decide: if a decision is easily reversible and its consequences stay contained, delegating it is not a problem. If it is hard to undo, or it commits the organisation beyond the person who made it, it should stay human. The Air Canada chatbot case, where a tribunal ordered the airline to honour a promise its own tool had invented, shows what happens when that line was never drawn. The answer is not to distrust AI. It is to write down, once, the short list of decisions the organisation refuses to hand over to it.

I was fortunate to spend much of my career close to senior executives. I learned a great deal alongside them — above all, that the difficulty almost never comes from a lack of information. It comes from the moment several valid signals point in different directions, and someone has to decide anyway, without certainty.

A strategic direction.

A major investment.

A reorganisation.

A decision that affects people.

What these four situations share is that no dashboard settles them on its own. Data can inform each one; none of them can be handed over entirely to a model.

A recent Morgan Stanley study on AI governance offers a useful insight: 41% of the executives surveyed see human oversight of high-risk situations as the most important governance rule. The fact that oversight ranks first among governance priorities is itself telling: even the organisations moving fastest on AI adoption keep the final call within reach of a human being on the decisions that matter most.

That figure, to me, points to the real question: how far should you delegate?

AI can analyse, compare, simulate, challenge an intuition and bring options to the surface.

How do you know if a decision can be delegated?

A simple test often settles it: if a decision is easily reversible and its consequences stay contained, delegating it is not a problem. If it is hard to undo, or its effects reach well beyond the person who made it, keeping it in human hands is not excessive caution — it is simply proportionate to the risk.

⚖️ Why must the final call stay human when a decision commits the company?

What did a real court case reveal about delegating to AI?

In February 2024, a Canadian tribunal ruled on a case that illustrates this risk well. An Air Canada customer had received a refund promise from the airline's own chatbot that did not match the company's actual policy. Air Canada argued before the tribunal that the chatbot should be treated as "a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions." The tribunal rejected that argument outright: a company remains responsible for what any of its agents say, human or automated. Air Canada was ordered to honour the promise.

The case says nothing about the chatbot's technical quality. It says that no one in the organisation held explicit responsibility for checking what the tool could commit the company to. The boundary did not exist before the incident. The tribunal drew it instead, in the least favourable direction.

How do you build that list, in practice?

In a mid-sized company, that list usually falls into four categories. Hiring and dismissing people in key roles, because a mistake there is rarely undone without a human cost. Commitments made to a customer beyond an amount or scope set in advance, precisely to avoid the chatbot scenario. Decisions that change the company's trajectory: a shift in offer, a new market, a change of business model. And capital allocation beyond a threshold each executive sets according to the size of their organisation.

What makes this list useful is not how exhaustive it is. It is that it exists before an urgent situation forces it to be improvised, and that everyone who might one day configure an AI tool knows it. A list only the executive knows about protects no one else in the organisation.

Because an executive does not decide with data alone. They decide within a context, with responsibility, and they answer for the consequences.

Give two experienced leaders the exact same data and the exact same AI-generated recommendation, and they can reach opposite decisions — both defensible — because each carries a context, a risk appetite and a set of relationships the model never sees. That is not a flaw in the tool. It is the reason the final call has to stay human. A model can hand both of them the same recommendation; only the executive later answers for the one they chose.

🎯 Where should the line sit between AI and human decision-making?

AI informs the decision. The executive keeps the final call. That line is not negotiated case by case — it gets drawn once, in a calm moment, before an urgent decision forces it to be improvised.

As AI advances, knowing what to delegate to it, and what never to delegate, will become, in my view, an executive skill. This is not a rejection of AI. It is a boundary drawn on purpose: AI narrows the options and stress-tests the reasoning; the executive still owns the decision, and answers for it.

Is this boundary fixed?

That boundary does not sit still. A decision that used to require human judgment can, over time and with enough evidence, move to what a model handles correctly on its own: a first pass of sorting cases, catching repetitive anomalies, drafting a summary. What does not change is the need to revisit it regularly, rather than fixing it once and forgetting about it.

The question I find myself asking most often is therefore not "do you trust AI," but "who in your organisation explicitly owns the right to say no to one of its recommendations?" If the answer is not clear, the boundary does not really exist yet — it is only assumed.

How do you formalise this boundary?

In practice, formalising that boundary rarely takes more than a short list: the categories of decisions the organisation refuses to delegate, whatever the quality of the tool. A written list, reviewed once a year, beats a general principle everyone interprets differently. Writing it down usually forces disagreements into the open that, left unspoken, would only have surfaced at the worst possible moment.

Who has the final say when the organisation disagrees?

A list of non-delegable decisions is not enough if no one knows who, internally, has the final word when two trusted people reach opposite conclusions. In a small structure, this is usually settled in practice: the executive decides, and that works well. The risk appears once the organisation grows, a leadership committee forms, and no one has explicitly redefined who arbitrates a disagreement on a decision of this kind.

The answer does not need to be complicated. It only needs to be written down, known to everyone, and set before the disagreement itself. An organisation that discovers mid-crisis that it never settled this question ends up settling it under pressure, often badly, and sometimes by letting an AI tool fill the gap by default — exactly the gap the Air Canada case made visible.
