Not every decision
can be delegated.
Not even to an AI.

Article developed from my LinkedIn post (8 September 2026)
Updated on 30 September 2026

Short version →

SME leaders: not every decision can be handed to an AI, however capable.

A simple test: if a decision is easy to undo and its consequences stay limited, you can hand it to AI.

If it is hard to put right, or it commits the company beyond the person who makes it, it stays human.

Air Canada was ordered to honour a promise its own chatbot had made up. Nobody had set the limit beforehand.

The way forward:

write down, once and for all, the short list of decisions you will never hand over to AI.

You run an SME and AI offers you a few more ready-made answers every day?
This piece is for you. I was lucky enough to spend much of my career alongside business leaders. I learned a great deal with them, especially about what makes a decision hard when the data does not all point the same way.

A strategic direction.
A major investment.
A reorganisation.
A decision that affects people.

On paper, these four situations have nothing in common. Yet they share one thing: their consequences reach far beyond the person who decides, and are still felt months, sometimes years later.

A recent Morgan Stanley study on AI governance caught my attention: 41% of the executives surveyed put human oversight of high-risk situations at the top of their governance rules.

To me, that figure is not about distrust. It shows that leaders already sense, instinctively, the difference between what can be delegated and what never should be.

AI can analyse, compare, simulate, shake up an intuition, bring options to light. That is already a lot.

How do you know if a decision can be delegated?

Ask yourself one question: if the decision turns out wrong, can we easily go back?
If yes, and the consequences stay limited, you can hand it to AI without worry. Sorting requests, suggesting a meeting slot, preparing a first summary: mistakes get fixed fast, and nobody is harmed.
If no, because the decision is hard to undo or its effects reach far beyond the person who made it, keep it in human hands. That is not excessive caution. It is in proportion to the risk.
When in doubt, also ask how long it would take to spot a mistake. An error seen in a minute does not call for the same vigilance as one a customer discovers six months later.

⚖️ Why must the final call stay human when a decision commits the company?

What did a real court case reveal about delegating to AI?

In February 2024, a Canadian tribunal ruled on a case that says it all. An Air Canada customer had been promised a refund by the airline’s chatbot. The problem: that promise did not match the company’s policy.
Before the tribunal, Air Canada argued its chatbot was “a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions”. The tribunal swept the argument aside: a company answers for what all its agents say, human or automated. Air Canada had to honour the promise.

What strikes me in this story is not the technology. It is that nobody in the company was in charge of checking what the tool could promise on the airline’s behalf. The limit did not exist before the incident. The tribunal set it instead, and not in the most favourable way.

How do you build that list, in practice?

In an SME, it often fits into four families.
One: hiring and dismissal in key roles. A mistake there is rarely repaired without human damage.
Two: commitments made to a client beyond an amount set in advance. That is exactly the chatbot scenario.
Three: decisions that change the company’s course, such as a new offer, a new country or a different business model.
Four: investments above a threshold each leader sets according to the size of the business.

This list does not need to be complete. It needs to exist before the emergency, and be known to everyone who might one day set up an AI tool. A list only the leader carries in their head protects nobody else.

Because a leader does not decide with figures alone. They decide within a context, with a responsibility, and they are the one who lives with the consequences.

Give two leaders exactly the same figures and they may, quite rightly, make opposite decisions. They are not committing the same company, or the same history. No model can carry that share of judgement for the person who will live with the outcome. A model may recommend the same option to both. The leader is the one who answers for the choice.

🎯 Where should the line sit between AI and human decision-making?

AI informs the decision. The leader decides. That limit is not negotiated case by case: it is set once, in a calm moment, before an emergency forces you to improvise.
This is not turning your back on it. It is recognising a simple fact: a tool, however good, will never be the one standing in front of the company to answer for what the decision produced.

The more AI advances, the more knowing what to hand over to it, and what never to hand over, will become a core leadership skill. I am convinced of it.

Is this boundary fixed?

No, and that is good news. A decision that needed human judgement yesterday can, with time and evidence, move over to what a tool handles well on its own: sorting a first level of files, spotting recurring anomalies, preparing a summary.
What does not change is the need to review the limit regularly, instead of setting it once and forgetting it. A limit nobody has reread in two years often describes a world that no longer exists.

So the question I would ask you is not “do you trust AI?”. It is rather: “who, at your company, clearly has the right to say no to what AI recommends?” If the answer is unclear, the limit does not exist yet. It is just assumed.

How do you formalise this boundary?

A short list is enough: the families of decisions the company refuses to hand over to AI, however good the tool. A written list, reread once a year, is worth more than a grand principle everyone reads their own way.
Writing it down has another benefit: it forces you to settle disagreements that, left vague, would only have surfaced at the worst possible moment.
One page is enough, with concrete examples: a dismissal, a price offered to a key client, a commitment to a partner.

Who has the final say when the organisation disagrees?

A list is not enough if nobody knows who has the last word when two trusted people reach opposite conclusions. In a small business, it is often settled by default: the leader decides, and that is fine. The risk comes when the company grows, an executive committee takes shape, and nobody restates clearly who arbitrates.

The answer is nothing complicated. It just needs to be written down, known to everyone, and decided before the disagreement. A company that discovers in the middle of a crisis that it never settled the question settles it in a rush, often badly. Sometimes an AI tool even fills the gap by default. That is exactly the gap the Air Canada case brought to light.

Delegate, assist, decide: three levels to tell apart

The debate is often framed as a choice: let AI decide, or do without it. In real life there are three levels, and most situations play out in the middle.

This is how I describe them:
delegate: the tool acts alone, and someone checks afterwards, from time to time;
assist: the tool prepares, and a person validates before anything happens;
decide: a person makes the call, the tool has only brought information.

For each type of decision, the right question is therefore not “AI or no AI”. It is “at which level”. A single process can even mix all three: an automatic sort, a prepared proposal, then a final human choice.

How do you assess the risk of a decision in a few minutes?

Four questions are enough, to ask before choosing the level:
if the decision is wrong, what happens, and to whom?
how long does it take to notice?
can the mistake be put right, and at what price?
does the decision commit the company to someone else, a customer, an employee or an authority?

The heavier the answers, the more the decision moves back to a person. An error you see at once and fix in a minute has nothing in common with an error a customer discovers six months later.

Who is responsible when an AI gets it wrong?

The company. That is what the Air Canada case recalled, and it is the logic of the law: a tool is responsible for nothing. Whoever uses it to act on their behalf answers for what it produces.

The European AI Act goes further for some uses classed as high-risk, such as recruitment or access to certain essential services. There it requires, among other things, genuine human oversight and informing the people concerned.

For a leader, the consequence is simple: as soon as a tool can commit the company, someone must be named to answer for what it does.

And decisions about people?

Here I would be especially careful. Assessing an employee, turning down a promotion, applying a sanction, choosing between two candidates: these decisions touch someone’s dignity and career.

The GDPR is clear on this: a person has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, when that decision has legal effects on them or significantly affects them. AI can help prepare, compare, spot something missed. The decision itself stays human, and the person concerned must be able to talk it through with someone.

What does a list look like, in practice?

An example for a service SME of about forty people:
every hire, every termination and every sanction;
every contractual commitment above an amount set by leadership;
every reply to a complaint that admits a fault or promises a goodwill gesture;
every public statement in the company’s name;
every change of prices or commercial terms;
every strategic choice discussed by the executive committee.

Six lines are enough. Each one covers a family of decisions, not a single case: the list stays valid even when the tools change.

Which decisions can you safely hand over to AI?

The ones that tick three boxes:
they come up often, so quality can be checked across many cases;
they follow known rules that can be written down in advance;
a single mistake costs little and is quickly fixed.

Sorting incoming requests, suggesting a meeting slot, flagging an unusual invoice: these decisions often gain from being handed to a tool, with regular checks. They free up time for the ones that deserve a person’s attention.

What if the AI and the expert disagree?

Do not decide too fast, in either direction. The disagreement is information: it reveals either a limit of the tool or a blind spot of the expert.

The most useful move is to ask each side to justify its position. The expert explains what they see that the data does not show. The tool, when asked, says what it relies on. Then the person responsible decides, and writes down what convinced them.

Over time, these recorded disagreements become a goldmine: they show where the tool is reliable, and where human experience remains essential.

And AI agents that act on their own?

Tools no longer just answer. Some send messages, place orders, change files. The question of delegation becomes very concrete.

Before handing an action to an agent, I would put three safeguards in place: a closed list of authorised actions, a ceiling above which human approval is required, and a log that shows who did what, the agent or a person. Without these three safeguards, the company is unknowingly delegating decisions it never chose.

The automation trap

A recommendation shown with confidence tends to be followed, even by competent people. The more often the tool is right, the more attention drops, and the more the rare mistake slips through.

You do not fight this with suspicion. You fight it with organisation. Rotating the people who review, setting a maximum number of approvals per day, rechecking an already approved file now and then: these simple habits keep attention where it matters.

How do you involve teams in drawing the line?

By asking them what they would never delegate. The people dealing with customers, suppliers or files know better than anyone where a mistake is costly.

A one-hour workshop, where everyone names a decision from their daily work and the level they would give it, often reveals risks leadership had not seen. And the teams feel, rightly, that the rule was built with them.

How do you train managers in this judgement?

With cases rather than principles. A manager learns more from looking at three real situations, where the tool was right, wrong or half right, than from reading a policy note.

It can become a short ritual in management meetings: one case a month, one simple question, “at which level would you have placed this decision?”. Judgement is built that way, through practice, like any leadership skill.

How do you talk about it with the board or the partners?

With a map rather than a speech. A table listing the main types of decisions, the level chosen for each and the person responsible answers most questions before anyone asks them.

Partners and board members are not asking the company to give up AI. They want to know that someone has thought about the limits, and that those limits are respected.

How do you review the line over time?

Once a year, with facts. For each type of delegated or assisted decision, look at the number of errors found, how serious they were and how long it took to spot them.

If errors are rare and minor, the decision can move down a level. If a serious error has appeared, it moves back up, long enough to understand what happened. The line stays alive, grounded in experience rather than the mood of the moment.

What this line says about a leadership team

Writing down what you do not delegate forces you to say what matters. It is an exercise in clear thinking as much as in caution.

A leadership team that knows where to draw the line can use AI more boldly everywhere else. Being clear about a few protected decisions frees up use on all the others.

How do you document a decision made with the help of an AI?

→

Keep a record of three things: the question asked, the options you looked at, and the reason for your choice. That short account lets you reread the decision later and understand what weighed.

No need to turn it into a file. A few lines written on the spot are worth more than a reconstruction after the fact. A paragraph in the meeting notes is often enough.

Add a fourth thing: what the AI had suggested and you did not keep. That is often where real judgement shows. It is also what an auditor or a client will ask you for first.

What does real human oversight consist of?

→

You need someone who reviews, at a precise moment, with the power to say no. Reviewing for form’s sake, with no power to stop anything, is not oversight. It is a signature.

The right question: at your company, who can turn down an AI recommendation, and under what conditions?

That person also needs the time to do it. A sign-off requested at the end of the day, across dozens of files, always ends up as a simple approval click. If you want real oversight, plan the time that goes with it.

How do you teach teams to challenge a recommendation?

→

Give them three questions. What does this recommendation rest on?
What could make it wrong?
What happens if we get it wrong?
They take five minutes to learn and work for everything.

With them, the AI’s answer becomes the start of the thinking, no longer its conclusion.

Practise on past cases whose ending you know. The team then sees where the AI would have been wrong, and learns to spot its blind spots without taking any risk.
After a few sessions, these questions become a team reflex rather than an instruction.

What place should be left for the leader’s intuition?

→

A real one, as long as you say it out loud. Intuition is often years of experience summed up in a second. Setting it aside in the name of data would be as questionable as following it without looking at the figures.

AI informs, intuition warns, and the leader decides, owning the choice.

A habit I like: write down your intuition before consulting the AI. A few weeks later, compare. You will learn a lot about how reliable each one is.
It is also the best way to learn when to listen to it.
