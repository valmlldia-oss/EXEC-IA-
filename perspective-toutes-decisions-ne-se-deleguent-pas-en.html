Not every decision
can be delegated.
Not even to an AI.

Expanded version of a LinkedIn post
September 8, 2026 · updated September 25, 2026

Short version →

Not every decision can be delegated to an AI, however capable.

A simple test helps decide: if a decision is easily reversible and its consequences stay contained, delegating it is not a problem.

If it is hard to undo, or it commits the organisation beyond the person who made it, it should stay human.

The Air Canada chatbot case, where a tribunal ordered the airline to honour a promise its own tool had invented, shows what happens when that line was never drawn.

The answer is not to distrust AI.

It is to write down, once, the short list of decisions the organisation refuses to hand over to it.

I was fortunate to spend much of my career close to senior executives. I learned a great deal alongside them, above all that the difficulty almost never comes from a lack of information. It comes from the moment several valid signals point in different directions, and someone has to decide anyway, without certainty.

A strategic direction.
A major investment.
A reorganisation.
A decision that affects people.

What these four situations share is that no dashboard settles them on its own. Data can inform each one; none of them can be handed over entirely to a model.

A recent Morgan Stanley study on AI governance offers a useful insight: 41% of the executives surveyed see human oversight of high-risk situations as the most important governance rule. The fact that oversight ranks first among governance priorities is itself telling: even the organisations moving fastest on AI adoption keep the final call within reach of a human being on the decisions that matter most.

That figure, to me, points to the real question: how far should you delegate?

AI can analyse, compare, simulate, challenge an intuition and bring options to the surface.

How do you know if a decision can be delegated?

A simple test often settles it: if a decision is easily reversible and its consequences stay contained, delegating it is not a problem. If it is hard to undo, or its effects reach well beyond the person who made it, keeping it in human hands is not excessive caution; it is simply proportionate to the risk.

⚖️ Why must the final call stay human when a decision commits the company?

What did a real court case reveal about delegating to AI?

In February 2024, a Canadian tribunal ruled on a case that illustrates this risk well. An Air Canada customer had received a refund promise from the airline's own chatbot that did not match the company's actual policy. Air Canada argued before the tribunal that the chatbot should be treated as "a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions." The tribunal rejected that argument outright: a company remains responsible for what any of its agents say, human or automated. Air Canada was ordered to honour the promise.

The case says nothing about the chatbot's technical quality. It says that no one in the organisation held explicit responsibility for checking what the tool could commit the company to. The boundary did not exist before the incident. The tribunal drew it instead, in the least favourable direction.

How do you build that list, in practice?

In a mid-sized company, that list usually falls into four categories. Hiring and dismissing people in key roles, because a mistake there is rarely undone without a human cost. Commitments made to a customer beyond an amount or scope set in advance, precisely to avoid the chatbot scenario. Decisions that change the company's trajectory: a shift in offer, a new market, a change of business model. And capital allocation beyond a threshold each executive sets according to the size of their organisation.

What makes this list useful is not how exhaustive it is. It is that it exists before an urgent situation forces it to be improvised, and that everyone who might one day configure an AI tool knows it. A list only the executive knows about protects no one else in the organisation.

Because an executive does not decide with data alone. They decide within a context, with responsibility, and they answer for the consequences.

Give two experienced leaders the exact same data and the exact same AI-generated recommendation, and they can reach opposite decisions (both defensible) because each carries a context, a risk appetite and a set of relationships the model never sees. That is not a flaw in the tool. It is the reason the final call has to stay human. A model can hand both of them the same recommendation; only the executive later answers for the one they chose.

🎯 Where should the line sit between AI and human decision-making?

AI informs the decision. The executive keeps the final call. That line is not negotiated case by case: it gets drawn once, in a calm moment, before an urgent decision forces it to be improvised.

As AI advances, knowing what to delegate to it, and what never to delegate, will become, in my view, an executive skill. This is not a rejection of AI. It is a boundary drawn on purpose: AI narrows the options and stress-tests the reasoning; the executive still owns the decision, and answers for it.

Is this boundary fixed?

That boundary does not sit still. A decision that used to require human judgment can, over time and with enough evidence, move to what a model handles correctly on its own: a first pass of sorting cases, catching repetitive anomalies, drafting a summary. What does not change is the need to revisit it regularly, rather than fixing it once and forgetting about it.

The question I find myself asking most often is therefore not "do you trust AI," but "who in your organisation explicitly owns the right to say no to one of its recommendations?" If the answer is not clear, the boundary does not really exist yet: it is only assumed.

How do you formalise this boundary?

In practice, formalising that boundary rarely takes more than a short list: the categories of decisions the organisation refuses to delegate, whatever the quality of the tool. A written list, reviewed once a year, beats a general principle everyone interprets differently. Writing it down usually forces disagreements into the open that, left unspoken, would only have surfaced at the worst possible moment.

Who has the final say when the organisation disagrees?

A list of non-delegable decisions is not enough if no one knows who, internally, has the final word when two trusted people reach opposite conclusions. In a small structure, this is usually settled in practice: the executive decides, and that works well. The risk appears once the organisation grows, a leadership committee forms, and no one has explicitly redefined who arbitrates a disagreement on a decision of this kind.

The answer does not need to be complicated. It only needs to be written down, known to everyone, and set before the disagreement itself. An organisation that discovers mid-crisis that it never settled this question ends up settling it under pressure, often badly, and sometimes by letting an AI tool fill the gap by default, exactly the gap the Air Canada case made visible.

Delegate, assist, decide: three levels to tell apart

The debate often pits two positions against each other: let AI decide, or do without it. In practice there are three levels, and most situations play out in the middle.

They can be described as follows:
delegate, when the tool acts alone and a person checks afterwards, by sampling;
assist, when the tool prepares and a person validates before any action;
decide, when a person makes the call, the tool having only provided analysis.

For each type of decision, the useful question is therefore not “AI or not” but “at which level”. A single process can even combine all three: automatic sorting, a prepared proposal, a final human choice.

How do you assess the risk of a decision in a few minutes?

Four questions are enough, asked before choosing the level:
what happens if the decision is wrong, and for whom?
how long does it take to notice?
can the mistake be corrected, and at what cost?
does the decision commit the company towards a third party, whether client, employee or authority?

The heavier the answers, the further the decision moves up towards the human level. A mistake visible at once and corrected in a minute does not call for the same vigilance as one discovered six months later by a client.

Who is responsible when an AI gets it wrong?

The company. That is what the Air Canada case recalled, and it is the general logic of the law: a tool has no responsibility of its own. Whoever uses it to act on their behalf answers for what it produces.

The European AI regulation reinforces this logic for certain uses classified as high-risk, such as recruitment or access to some essential services. It requires, among other things, effective human oversight and information for the people concerned.

For a leader, the consequence is simple: whenever a tool can commit the company, someone must be designated to answer for what it does.

And decisions that concern people?

They deserve particular vigilance. Appraising an employee, refusing a promotion, taking disciplinary action, choosing between two candidates: these decisions touch someone’s dignity and career.

The GDPR sets a clear principle: a person has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing when that decision produces legal effects or significantly affects them. AI can help prepare, compare and spot an omission. The decision itself remains human, and the person concerned must be able to discuss it with someone.

What does a list look like in practice?

Here is an example for a service SME of around forty people:
every hire, every termination and every disciplinary decision;
any contractual commitment above an amount set by leadership;
any reply to a complaint that admits fault or promises compensation;
any public communication on behalf of the company;
any change of prices or commercial terms;
any strategic choice discussed by the executive committee.

Six lines are enough. Each one designates a family of decisions, not a single case, so that the list stays valid when tools change.

Which decisions can safely be entrusted to AI?

Those that combine three characteristics:
they recur often, which makes it possible to check quality over a large number of cases;
they follow known rules that can be written down in advance;
an isolated mistake costs little and is quickly corrected.

Sorting incoming requests, suggesting an appointment slot, flagging an unusual invoice: these decisions often benefit from being entrusted to a tool, under regular checks. They free up time for those that truly deserve a person’s attention.

What should you do when the AI and the expert disagree?

Avoid settling it too quickly either way. The disagreement is information: it signals either a limit of the tool or a blind spot of the expert.

The most useful approach is to ask each side to justify its position. The expert explains what they see that the data does not show; the tool, when questioned, sets out the elements it relies on. The decision then rests with the person in charge, who notes what convinced them.

Over time, these documented disagreements become a resource: they show where the tool is reliable, and where human experience remains indispensable.

And AI agents that act on their own?

Tools no longer just answer: some send messages, place orders or modify files. The question of delegation then becomes concrete.

Before entrusting an action to an agent, three safeguards are needed: a closed list of authorised actions, a ceiling above which human validation is required, and a log that shows who, the agent or a person, did what. Without these three elements, the company unknowingly delegates decisions it never chose.

The automation trap

A recommendation displayed with confidence tends to be followed, even by competent people. The more often the tool is right, the more vigilance drops, and the more the rare mistake goes unnoticed.

This is not countered by distrust, but by organisation. Rotating the people who review, setting a maximum number of validations per day, occasionally re-checking a file already approved: these simple habits keep attention where it matters.

How do you involve teams in drawing the line?

By asking them what they would never delegate. People dealing with clients, suppliers or files know better than anyone where a mistake is costly.

A one-hour workshop, in which everyone names a decision from their daily work and the level they would assign to it, often reveals risks leadership had not seen. It also gives teams the accurate sense that the rule was built with them.

How do you train managers in this judgement?

Through cases rather than principles. A manager learns more by examining three real situations, where a recommendation from the tool was right, wrong or incomplete, than by reading a policy note.

The exercise can become a short ritual in management meetings: one case a month, one simple question, “at which level would you have placed this decision?”. Judgement is built through practice, like any leadership skill.

How do you discuss it with the board or the partners?

With a map rather than a speech. A table listing the main types of decisions, the level chosen for each and the person responsible answers most questions before they are asked.

Partners and board members do not ask the company to give up AI. They want to know that someone has thought about the limits, and that those limits are respected.

How do you review the line over time?

Once a year, with facts. For each type of delegated or assisted decision, look at the number of mistakes recorded, their severity and the time taken to detect them.

If mistakes are rare and minor, the decision can move down a level. If a serious mistake has occurred, it moves up, while the cause is understood. The line thus stays alive, grounded in experience rather than in the intuition of the moment.

What this line says about a leadership team

Writing down what you do not delegate forces you to say what really matters. It is an exercise in lucidity as much as in caution.

A leadership team that knows where to draw the line can use AI more boldly elsewhere. Clarity on a few protected decisions frees up use on all the others.

How do you document a decision made with the help of an AI?

→

By keeping a record of three things: the question asked, the options examined and the reason for the final choice. This short account lets you reread the decision later and understand what mattered.

It does not need to become a file: a few lines, written at the time of the decision, are worth more than an after-the-fact reconstruction.

Add a fourth element: what the AI suggested and you did not keep. That is often where real judgement shows, and it is what an auditor or a client will ask for first.

What does real human oversight consist of?

→

It requires someone who reviews, at a precise moment, with the power to say no. A purely formal review, with no power to stop, is not oversight: it is a signature.

The useful question is therefore: who in the organisation can refuse an AI recommendation, and under what conditions?

That person also needs the time to do it. A sign-off requested at the end of the day, across dozens of files, mechanically becomes a click of approval.

How do you teach teams to challenge a recommendation?

→

By giving them three questions: what does this recommendation rest on, what could make it wrong, and what happens if we are mistaken? These questions are learned quickly and apply to everything.

They turn the AI’s answer into the starting point of a reflection, not a conclusion.

Practise on past cases whose outcome you know: the team then sees where the AI would have been wrong, and learns to spot its blind spots without taking any risk.

What place should be left for the leader’s intuition?

→

A real one, provided it is spoken. Intuition often sums up long experience; setting it aside in the name of data would be as questionable as following it without looking at the data.

AI can inform, intuition can warn: the leader decides, owning what they choose.

A good habit: write down your intuition before consulting the AI. Comparing the two a few weeks later says a great deal about how reliable each one is.
