Can your AI project be funded?
Yes, but not necessarily
the one you have in mind.

Article developed from my LinkedIn post (25 September 2026)

Short version →

SME leaders: yes, an AI project can be funded. But not necessarily yours, not in every company, and not for every kind of service.

The Diag Data IA scheme funds a technical analysis, carried out by an approved expert.

The Osez l’IA plan lists ready-made solutions and approved experts. Not open consulting.

A subsidy should never decide your project: first the problem to solve, then the funding.

You run an SME and you wonder whether your AI project could be funded?
This piece is for you.
It all started with an audit of my site’s visibility on Google and in AI answers. One question came up: what are my future clients actually asking themselves?

Among them: “Is there any funding for my AI project?” Good question. So I went and took a closer look.

Osez l’IA, France 2030, Bpifrance, Diag Data IA, France Num, regional schemes… The funding exists. But behind the word “support”, the realities are very different: eligible company or not, fundable service or not, free choice of provider or imposed, share covered, amount left to pay.

💶 Should funding determine your AI project?

No, never. I always keep this order:

  • what problem do you want to solve?
  • Where can AI really create value?
  • Which project should be prioritised?
  • And only then: which schemes may apply?

A project built to fit a scheme risks solving no real problem.
You would then have spent time, and part of the money, on a result nobody was waiting for. Funding should speed up a project you have already chosen, never choose it for you.

What does Diag Data IA fund?

For SMEs and mid-sized companies with 10 to 2,000 employees, Bpifrance’s IA Booster programme, part of France 2030, first offers free awareness sessions. Then a Diag Data IA co-funded at 40%: €10,000 excl. VAT, of which €6,000 excl. VAT is paid by the company.
It is a technical diagnosis, carried out by a Bpifrance-approved expert. Conditions apply, including turnover of at least €1M and being an independent company.

So you do not freely choose the provider. And this diagnosis does not replace the strategic thinking upstream: which use to prioritise, and why that one.

Which main families of support exist for an AI project?

There are three, each with its own logic.
Diagnostic schemes fund an analysis, such as the Diag Data IA.
Solution catalogues list ready-to-use products, such as one of the two strands of the Osez l’IA plan.
Broader support, national or regional, backs digital transformation or investment.

They do not replace one another. Confusing a funded diagnosis with a funded rollout is one of the most common mistakes.
Before filling in an application, check which family it belongs to: that tells you what will really be funded, and what will stay at your expense.

Why is the provider question decisive?

Because, depending on the scheme, you either choose your provider freely, or they come from a list.
In the first case, you pick the one who understands your business.
In the second, the choice is framed: check that the proposed provider meets your need.

Funding obtained for a provider who does not fit the project costs you time, even when it costs you no money.

How do you combine a funded diagnosis with a strategic project?
By keeping each in its place. The technical diagnosis shows what is possible with your data and your tools. The strategic thinking decides what is worth doing, in what order, with what means.

Ideally, strategy comes first. A diagnosis targeted on a use already chosen brings far more than a general diagnosis with no precise question.

Should you wait for funding before starting?

Not necessarily. Waiting for support can delay by several months a project whose value is already clear. Meanwhile, the problem stays untouched.
The right question is not “support or no support”. It is: “what first step can I take right now, whatever the support?”.

A modest first use, on a precise task, teaches you things no application file can. It also makes the next step easier to defend, including in front of a funding body.

Does “Osez l’IA” fund any kind of consulting?
No. The national Osez l’IA plan has two strands.
The first is a catalogue of ready-to-use AI solutions for SMEs and mid-sized companies: assistants, automation, document analysis. These are products.
The second groups support programmes run by Bpifrance, such as the Diag Data IA and the AI Accelerator, delivered by approved experts.

In both cases, you do not freely choose the provider. A strategic scoping workshop such as Cap IA is neither a catalogue product nor an approved-expert service: it does not fall under these schemes, and I do not present it as fundable by them.
EXEC’IA’s AI Agents, on the other hand, are product solutions: SENTRY, ECHO or RELAY could fit into a catalogue of this kind, provided they are selected.

Which questions should you ask before filing an application?

Five checks save you from unpleasant surprises:

  • is your company eligible (size, turnover, sector)?
  • Is the intended service fundable?
  • Can you freely choose your provider, or is it imposed?
  • What is the funding rate?
  • What is the out-of-pocket cost, and for what result?

The exact conditions are published by the bodies themselves (France Num, Bpifrance, entreprises.gouv.fr), and they change. Check them at the source, when you apply.

Where do you start, in practice?
With the problem, not the funding desk.
Write down what AI should change in your business, find where it creates value, choose a first project.
Funding comes next, based on that project.

Funding and compliance should both be anticipated at the scoping stage: for the second, see the AI Act and GDPR guide for an SME.

Which mistakes should you avoid when looking for support?

Five mistakes come up again and again:

  • building a project to fit a scheme,
  • ignoring the out-of-pocket cost,
  • confusing diagnosis and deployment,
  • forgetting compliance (AI Act, GDPR) at the scoping stage, and
  • starting too late.

The last one deserves checking every time: depending on the scheme, the application sometimes has to be made before the service starts. It is written in the official conditions. Read them before committing.

What if your company is not eligible?
Do not give up on the project.
An ineligible company can fund a first use itself, starting small: one task, one team, one measurable result.
It is even the best way to find out whether the project deserves a bigger investment.

Having no support is not a verdict on the project’s value.
It only changes the pace and the size of the first step.

How do you keep your bearings?
Among so many schemes

By coming back to the starting question: what problem do you want to solve?
Schemes are many, their conditions change, and so does their vocabulary.
Your problem stays the same. That is what should guide the choice.

A leader who knows which problem they want to solve reads a list of funding schemes as a list of options.
One who does not know yet reads it as a list of constraints.

Which three things should you keep in mind?
The project must exist without the support, even on a smaller scale.
Conditions are checked at the source, on the date you apply.
Compliance is thought through from the start, not after the rollout.

If these three points are settled, the support becomes what it should be:
an accelerator for a project already chosen, not the reason for choosing it.

What place for strategic support before funding?

A place upstream, to answer a question funding schemes rarely address:
where can AI create value in your company, and where do you start?
That is the role of a scoping workshop such as Cap IA at EXEC’IA.

Once the problem and the priority are clear, the funded diagnosis, the chosen solution and any support fall into place much more simply.
You choose with a clear direction, instead of picking from a menu.

How do you present your project to a funding body?
Problem before solution: what you want to change, for whom, and how you will know it worked.
A funding body pays for an expected result more than for an intention.
An application built on a clear need, a limited scope and a simple success criterion is quicker to read and easier to defend.

Also prepare your answers to three questions: who will carry the project internally, how much time it will take, and what you will do if the result disappoints.
Those answers reassure as much as a figure.

What do you do once the funding is granted?

Funding pays for a step, not for what follows.
Plan from the start for what happens next: who uses the result, who develops it, and how you decide to continue.
A diagnosis left in a drawer changes nothing in the business.

Go back to the starting order: problem, value, priority. It will tell you whether the funded step was the right one, and what the next one is.
Keep every document too: invoices, deliverables, meeting notes. A funding body may ask for them, sometimes months later.

What a first project really costs

Before looking for funding, you need to know what you want to fund. The listed price of a tool is often only part of the spending.

Six items make up the real budget of a first project:
scoping, to choose the use and set the indicator;
licences or subscriptions, for the planned number of users;
connection to existing tools, where needed;
team training, at the start and over time;
human review time, as long as the tool can get things wrong;
internal time spent on the project, by the leader and the teams.

The last item is the one most often forgotten. Yet it weighs the most in an SME: every hour spent on the project is an hour taken from somewhere else.

How do you estimate a project’s return, simply?

By comparing two figures over one year: what the project costs in total, and what it earns or saves, measured on the indicator chosen at the start.

For a leader, a payback period often speaks louder than a percentage: after how many months has the project paid back what it cost?
If the answer is longer than the tool’s likely lifespan, rethink the project before funding it.

Training, often easier to fund than the tool

It is an option people often forget. In France, staff training can be covered by the company’s skills operator, the OPCO, depending on its size and the rules of its sector.

Training is often the key to getting a tool adopted. Funding skills first, then the tool, is also the order that works best: teams that understand what they are going to use, and why.

What about regional support?

Several French regions support the digital transformation of small businesses and SMEs: vouchers, grants, interest-free loans or group programmes. Conditions differ from one region to another, and change often.

The chamber of commerce, the regional economic development agency or your local France Num adviser usually know what is open at the time you call. Fifteen minutes on the phone saves hours of searching.

Are tax credits a good fit?

Rarely for simply using a tool. Research and innovation tax credits target design and development work, not the purchase of off-the-shelf software.

They can make sense if the company develops a new solution itself, for example a custom tool built on its own data. In that case, ask your accountant before you start: you will avoid unpleasant surprises in an audit.

Subscription or investment?

The question looks like accounting. In reality, it is strategic. A subscription is paid monthly, easy to stop and counts as an expense. Custom development needs an upfront investment, but can become a company asset.

For a first project, a subscription is often the wisest choice: it lets you test at a controlled cost, and stop if the use does not deliver. Investment makes sense once the use is proven.

Should you involve your accountant?

Yes, and early. Several practical questions are theirs, and they are easier to settle before the first invoice than after.

Three examples come up often:
subscriptions billed by foreign vendors, for which VAT is usually self-assessed by the French company;
how spending is recorded, as an expense or as an asset, depending on whether it is a subscription or a development;
possible eligibility for a tax credit, which depends on the real nature of the work.

Half an hour is often enough. It avoids costly corrections, and gives you a clear view of what the project really costs, tax included.

Subscriptions in dollars

Many AI tools are billed in dollars. The amount in euros therefore moves with the exchange rate, and the bank may add fees to every payment.

For an annual budget, allow a margin of a few per cent. Some vendors offer billing in euros or an annual payment: simpler to manage, and often cheaper overall.

What about several companies together?

It is an underrated route. Business clubs, trade federations and chambers of commerce sometimes run group programmes: workshops, shared diagnoses, small-group training.

The cost per company goes down, and talking with leaders facing the same questions is often worth as much as the content itself. For a small business, it is an accessible first step before a project of its own.

How do you present a convincing application?

Whether for a public body, a bank or your own executive committee, four elements are often enough:
the problem to solve, described with the company’s own figures;
the use chosen, and why that one rather than another;
the indicator that will judge the result;
the full budget and the timeline, with the decision points.

An application that is clear on these four points stands out straight away. Most requests describe a tool. Few describe the expected result.

Beware of “free” offers paid for by a vendor

Some offers present themselves as free or funded, when their main purpose is to install a solution that is hard to leave afterwards. The cost comes later, at renewal or the day you want to switch tools.

Before accepting, three questions are enough: what happens when the free period ends?
Can the data be retrieved easily?
Does the tool answer the priority problem?

Timelines, a factor to plan for

Public schemes have processing times that can take several weeks. Some require the application to be filed before anything starts.

The right reflex: run both in parallel. Prepare the application while you refine the scoping, without committing any spending that would make the project ineligible. The funding timeline should not dictate the project’s timeline, but you should not discover it too late either.

What if the application is turned down?

Do not conclude that the project is bad. A refusal often comes down to an administrative criterion: company size, sector, timing, a budget already used up.

Ask for the exact reason. If it is about the application, it tells you what to clarify. If it is about eligibility, it invites you to look for another scheme, or to fund a modest first step yourself, whose results will make the next application stronger.

A well-understood refusal often saves time: it forces you to pin down the problem, the indicator and the budget. That is exactly what the project needed anyway.

A typical example

Take a thirty-person SME that wants to cut the time spent preparing quotes. After a short scoping, it chooses a precise use, sets an indicator, the average response time, and works out its full budget.

It gets the sales team’s training funded by its OPCO, takes out a subscription for a three-month trial, and prepares in parallel an application to its region for the next step. Three months later, it has real figures: the next step is easier to fund, and easier to decide.

What to remember

Funding follows clarity, never the other way round. A company that knows which problem it wants to solve, with which indicator and which budget, finds the right schemes more easily, and above all the right partners.

One that looks for funding first, then a project to justify it, loses time for an uncertain result. The order matters more than the amount.
