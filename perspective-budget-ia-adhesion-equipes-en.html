The AI budget is not the issue.
Buy-in is.

September 25, 2026

Short version →

A generous AI budget does not guarantee that teams will use the tool.

A HubSpot study of marketers shows a wide gap between company investment and employees’ appetite for using it.

Three levers make the difference: explain before equipping, train on everyday uses, and prove a concrete result on a small scope before scaling up.

A generous budget approved without discussion. Teams that do not move at the same pace. I have seen this scenario in executive committees, long before AI became the topic.

A HubSpot study, which I came across in 2026, confirms it: 98% of companies planned to maintain or increase their AI investment in 2025. Only half of their employees (51%) wanted to use it.

The study covers marketers. Read it as a signal, not as a statistic that holds for every function. But the signal is clear: the budget is not the issue. Buy-in is.

🔑 Why does buy-in weigh more than budget?

Because a tool never changes an organisation on its own. Teams decide every day whether to use it or work around it.

Resistance almost never comes from the technology. It comes from the habits the tool is asked to replace.

What does the gap between investment and willingness to use the tool reveal?

Two clocks run in parallel. The first is the clock of the decision: a budget is voted in a few weeks, a tool is rolled out in a few months. The second is the clock of habits: they change at the pace of the teams, not of the executive committee’s calendar.

When these two clocks are not synchronised, the organisation spends money on a use that does not exist yet. The dashboard says the tool is deployed. The reality on the ground says it is opened from time to time, then forgotten.

Why is the budget spent a poor indicator?

Because it measures an intention, not a change. A budget spent proves that a decision was taken. It says nothing about what teams do differently on Monday morning compared with yesterday.

The right indicator is regular use: how many people use the tool each week, for which precise task, and what have they stopped doing by hand?

How do you measure buy-in, in practice?
Three questions are enough to start.

  • Who uses it every week, and for what?
  • Who tried it and gave up, and why?
  • Which task has really disappeared or become shorter?

These answers come from a twenty-minute conversation with a few team members, not from a satisfaction form. They give a truer picture than any login statistic.

Why does a team adopt a tool?

A team adopts a tool when it sees the benefit for itself, not only for the company. The gain must be felt in daily work: a shorter task, one less error, a file prepared faster.

When the benefit is only visible on a leadership dashboard, the tool is experienced as an extra burden. The first job is therefore to translate the expected benefit into the language of the people who will use it.

Which forms of resistance come up most often?
Three come back:
The fear of getting it wrong, especially when the tool seems to leave no room for error.
The fear of being replaced, which is rarely voiced openly.
The impression that the tool adds a step instead of removing one.

Each calls for a different answer: a right to try, a clear word from leadership about what will not change, and a demonstration on a real task rather than on a catalogue example.

Why explain before equipping?

🎯 Which levers really make the difference?
Three levers, from the simplest to the most demanding: explain before equipping, train on real uses, prove early.

The why always comes before the tool. A team that understands what the tool changes in its daily work, and what it does not change, adopts it more readily than a team told that a rollout is coming.

How do you train without overwhelming teams?
By starting from everyday uses, not from the list of features. One hour on a task a person does every week is worth more than a day of general demonstration.

When should the tool be extended to the whole organisation?

After proving a concrete result, early, on a limited scope. That result becomes the argument, in place of the promise. The rollout then reaches teams who have already seen what the tool delivers.

What should you do when a team works around the tool?
Start by understanding, before restating the rule. A workaround is almost never ill will. It reveals a need the tool does not cover, a step that is too heavy, or a habit nobody helped to change.

A team that works around a tool is telling you, without saying it, what it expects from it. That is valuable information, provided you gather it without hunting for culprits.

What role does the leader play in buy-in?

A role of demonstration more than of decision. A leader who uses the tool personally, who says what it is useful for and where it disappointed, gives teams permission to try and to say what does not work.

Conversely, a tool imposed without being used by those who impose it sends a message that contradicts the speech.

Which mistakes should you avoid in a rollout?
Four mistakes come back: deploying everywhere at once, training on the list of features, announcing without explaining the why, and judging success by the budget consumed rather than by real use.

Each has the same origin: treating the tool as a purchase to succeed rather than as a change of practice to support.

How do you run a thirty-day pilot?

Week one: choose a volunteer team and a task that recurs every week, then explain why.
Week two: train on that task only.
Week three: let the team use it and note what gets in the way.
Week four: measure the result and decide to continue, adjust or stop.

At the end of the month, the leader has something the budget alone never provided: proof, inside their own organisation, of what the tool brings or does not bring.

What role should line managers play?
A central one. They turn the decision into daily practice. A convinced manager sets the example, protects learning time and reports what gets in the way.
A manager who was not involved becomes, unintentionally, the first brake.

Before rolling out, it is worth holding a short session for these relays: what changes for them, what they will tell their teams, and whom they will turn to when a difficulty appears.

How do you know whether buy-in is growing?

Three concrete signs:
the same people come back to the tool from one week to the next;
they mention it unprompted when they talk about their work;
they suggest new uses themselves.

Conversely, use concentrated on one or two people, or vanishing as soon as the coaching stops, signals surface-level buy-in. These signals are picked up by observation and conversation, with no extra measurement tool.

What should you tell a leader who wants to go fast?
That speed is won through buy-in, not haste.
A fast rollout to a team that has not bought in costs time twice: to install it, then to restart it. A short pilot with a visible result convinces faster than a communication plan.

The right timetable is one where each step proves something to the next team.

What role do the first users play?

The first convinced users are the best relays. Their feedback, in their own words, carries more weight than a presentation from leadership. Identify them early, give them a little time to go deeper, and let them show colleagues what works and what does not.

Take care not to turn them into isolated experts: their role is to share, not to carry the use alone for the whole team.

How do you keep momentum after launch?
A use that is no longer animated fades. Plan a short, regular meeting to share what worked, what got in the way and what each person tried. Fifteen minutes is enough, provided it actually happens.

Highlight a concrete result rather than a usage figure: a task shortened, a file delivered earlier. That is what other teams will remember.

Every team has its own rhythm, and comparing one team with another rarely helps. What matters is that each one can see, in its own work, what has changed since the tool arrived, and that someone listens when it says what still gets in the way. That listening is what keeps the tool alive after the launch effect has faded.

Four attitudes towards a new tool

In every team, reactions fall into roughly the same pattern. Recognising them lets you adapt how you support each person, instead of giving everyone the same speech.

Four attitudes often appear:
the enthusiasts, who have already tried it and want to go faster;
the cautious, who wait to see concrete results;
the sceptics, who doubt its value and say so;
the worried, who fear for their job or their competence, often without saying so.

Enthusiasts become champions, provided they are not left on their own. The cautious are convinced by proof. Sceptics deserve to be heard, because they often spot real flaws. The worried need, first of all, to be reassured about their future.

Age is not the issue

It is often said that younger people adopt AI more easily. In practice, buy-in depends far more on perceived usefulness than on generation.

An experienced employee who sees the tool save an hour on a tedious task adopts it quickly. A young employee who does not see what it is for turns away from it. Thinking in terms of age leads to poor training choices, and sometimes to hurtful blunders.

Words that reassure, words that worry

The vocabulary of the announcement matters more than people think. “Replace”, “automate jobs”, “productivity gains”: these words, even without bad intent, are heard as a threat.

“Lighten”, “free up time for”, “help you to” often describe the same reality, but from the point of view of the person doing the work. Choosing words is not cosmetic communication: it acknowledges that the person opposite has something to lose, and takes that seriously.

Protecting learning time

Many roll-outs fail for a simple reason: teams are asked to learn a tool on top of their work, without being given the time.

Setting aside a short, regular slot, for example one hour a week during the first month, changes everything. This protected time also sends a message: the company considers learning part of the job, not an extra effort expected in the evening.

A shared library of good instructions

Every user eventually finds the phrasings that produce good results. If they stay in people’s heads, the organisation keeps relearning the same thing.

A simple shared library gathers these instructions by task: preparing meeting notes, rewording a letter, summarising a tender. Everyone can draw on it and contribute. It speeds up adoption for newcomers and recognises those who found the best practices.

Should usage be rewarded?

Recognised, yes; paid per use, no. A bonus tied to the number of log-ins or requests pushes people to use the tool for the figure, not the result, and distorts the indicators leadership looks at.

The most effective recognition is often the simplest: mentioning a success in a meeting, giving someone the role of champion, giving them time to help colleagues. What motivates lastingly is being seen as useful.

How do you welcome newcomers?

By including AI tools in the onboarding path, just like business software. A new employee who discovers in their first week the uses in place, the library of instructions and the rules to follow adopts good practice from the outset.

A buddy who is already comfortable with these tools speeds up integration further. The newcomer learns the team’s real uses, not only those described in documentation.

Showcasing internal successes

Nothing convinces more than a colleague. An example from a neighbouring team, with its figures and its limits, speaks louder than an international study or a vendor demo.

A short monthly share, five minutes in a meeting, where someone shows what the tool has brought them, gradually builds a culture. Trials that did not work should be included too: they show that it is acceptable to get things wrong.

The right to say it does not work

Buying in does not mean obeying. A team that can report that a tool does not suit it, without being suspected of ill will, provides valuable information.

Providing a simple channel for this feedback, and responding to it, changes the relationship. Teams see that their opinion counts, and leadership avoids persisting with a tool nobody really uses.

What role for human resources?

A supporting role, often underestimated. HR can include AI use in annual reviews, identify training needs and spot people who are struggling before they drop out.

HR can also watch a sensitive point: when a tool saves time, what happens to that time? If it turns only into extra workload, buy-in erodes. If it is used for more interesting tasks, buy-in grows.

And with remote teams?

Adoption follows the same rules, with one added risk: isolation. Alone in front of a screen, an employee who gets stuck gives up faster, with no neighbour to ask.

Short video sessions, pairs who help each other and a written space for questions, where nobody fears looking behind, largely make up for the distance.

And when the tool changes?

AI tools keep evolving: new interface, new version, a feature moved or removed. Each change can break a habit that has only just formed, and cost the benefit of the first weeks.

The designated champion plays a valuable role here: following the changes, warning the team of what is different, updating the library of instructions and offering a short refresher if needed. An announced change is absorbed in a few days; an imposed one can make people abandon the tool.

What should you do about a lasting refusal?

Start with a one-to-one conversation, without pressure. A persistent refusal often hides a precise reason: a bad first experience, a task the tool does not serve, a worry about the future of the role.

In many cases, the use is not essential for everyone, and time can be given. When it becomes essential, for example because a whole process depends on it, this must be explained clearly, with more support and a realistic deadline. Forcing the issue rarely produces real buy-in.

How long before a use becomes a habit?

Several weeks of regular practice, generally. The first days are about curiosity; the habit forms when the tool is used for a real task, several times a week.

Two checkpoints help follow this shift: after one month, to fix what gets in the way, and after three months, to check that the use holds once the launch attention has faded.

A typical example

Take a forty-person SME that bought licences for everyone, with no result after three months. Rather than adding general training, leadership starts by listening: a short conversation with each team reveals unspoken worries and two uses that already work.

It protects one hour a week, creates a library of instructions based on these two uses and asks the people who found them to present them. Three months later, regular use has clearly increased, with no extra budget. What was missing was not the tool, but the way it was supported.

What buy-in really demands

Consideration more than conviction. You do not win a team’s buy-in by explaining that the tool is good, but by showing that you have understood what it changes for them.

A budget buys licences; only attention to people buys their willingness to use them.
