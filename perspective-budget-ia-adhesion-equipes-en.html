The AI budget is not the issue.
Buy-in is.

September 25, 2026

Short version →

Business leaders: a generous AI budget does not guarantee that your teams will use the tool.

A HubSpot study of marketers shows a clear gap between what companies invest and how much employees actually want to use the tool.

Three levers make the difference: explain before equipping, train on everyday uses, prove a concrete result on a small scope before scaling up.

You run a company and you have just approved an AI budget?
This piece is for you.
A generous budget, approved without discussion. Teams that do not move at the same pace. I have seen this scenario in executive committees, long before AI became the topic.

A HubSpot study confirms it: 98% of companies planned to maintain or increase their AI investment in 2025. Only half of their employees, 51%, actually wanted to use it.

The study covers marketers. I read it as a signal, not as a statistic that holds for every job. But the signal is clear: the budget is not the issue. Buy-in is.

🔑 Why does buy-in weigh more than budget?

Because a tool never changes an organisation on its own. Your teams decide every day whether to use it or work around it.
You can sign the best contract on the market: if nobody opens the tool on Monday morning, you have bought a licence, not a change.

Resistance almost never comes from the technology. It comes from the habits the tool is asked to replace.
A habit is years of gestures that work. You do not replace it with an announcement. You replace it with a better way of working that the team has tested for itself.

What does the gap between investment and willingness to use the tool reveal?

Two clocks run in parallel.
The first is the clock of the decision: a budget is approved in a few weeks, a tool is rolled out in a few months.
The second is the clock of habits: they change at the pace of the teams, not at the pace of the executive committee’s diary.

When these two clocks are not set together, the company pays for a use that does not exist yet. The dashboard says the tool is deployed. The field says it is opened now and then, then forgotten.
My advice: before talking budget, ask how long your teams will need to change their habits. It usually takes longer than the rollout.

Why is the budget spent a poor indicator?

Because it measures an intention, not a change. A budget spent proves that a decision was taken. It says nothing about what teams do differently on Monday morning compared with yesterday.

The right indicator is regular use: how many people use the tool each week, for which precise task, and what have they stopped doing by hand?

How do you measure buy-in, in practice?
Three questions are enough to start.

  • Who uses it every week, and for what?
  • Who tried it and gave up, and why?
  • Which task has really disappeared or become shorter?

These answers come from a twenty-minute conversation with a few team members, not from a satisfaction form. They give a truer picture than any login statistic.

Why does a team adopt a tool?

A team adopts a tool when it sees the benefit for itself, not only for the company. The gain must be felt in daily work: a shorter task, one less error, a file prepared faster.

When the benefit is only visible on a leadership dashboard, the tool is experienced as an extra burden. The first job is therefore to translate the expected benefit into the language of the people who will use it.

Which forms of resistance come up most often?
Three come back.
1) The fear of getting it wrong, especially when the tool seems to leave no room for error.
2) The fear of being replaced, which is rarely said out loud.
3) The feeling that the tool adds a step instead of removing one.

Each one calls for its own answer: a right to try, a clear word from leadership about what will not change, and a demonstration on a real task rather than a catalogue example.

Why explain before equipping?

🎯 Which levers really make the difference?
Three, from the simplest to the most demanding.
1) Explain before equipping.
2) Train on real uses.
3) Prove early.

The why always comes before the tool. A team that understands what the tool changes in its daily work, and what it does not, adopts it more readily than a team that is simply told about a rollout.
Explaining takes one meeting. Not explaining costs months of silent resistance.

How do you train without overwhelming teams?
Start from everyday uses, not from the list of features. One hour on a task a person does every week is worth more than a full day of general demonstration.

When should the tool be extended to the whole organisation?

After proving a concrete result, early, on a limited scope. That result becomes the argument, in place of the promise. The rollout then reaches teams who have already seen what the tool delivers.

What should you do when a team works around the tool?
Start by understanding, before restating the rule. A workaround is almost never ill will. It reveals a need the tool does not cover, a step that is too heavy, or a habit nobody helped to change.

A team that works around a tool is telling you, without saying it, what it expects from it. That is valuable information, provided you gather it without hunting for culprits.

What role does the leader play in buy-in?

A role of demonstration more than of decision. A leader who uses the tool personally, who says what it is useful for and where it disappointed, gives teams permission to try and to say what does not work.

Conversely, a tool imposed without being used by those who impose it sends a message that contradicts the speech.

Which mistakes should you avoid in a rollout?
Four come back.
1) Deploying everywhere at once.
2) Training on the list of features.
3) Announcing without explaining the why.
4) Judging success by the budget spent rather than by real use.

Each has the same origin: treating the tool as a purchase to succeed rather than as a change of practice to support.

How do you run a thirty-day pilot?

Week one: choose a volunteer team and a task that recurs every week, then explain why.
Week two: train on that task only.
Week three: let the team use it and note what gets in the way.
Week four: measure the result and decide to continue, adjust or stop.

At the end of the month, the leader has something the budget alone never provided: proof, inside their own organisation, of what the tool brings or does not bring.

What role should line managers play?
A central one. They turn the decision into daily practice. A convinced manager sets the example, protects learning time and reports what gets in the way.
A manager who was not involved becomes, unintentionally, the first brake.

Before rolling out, it is worth holding a short session for these relays: what changes for them, what they will tell their teams, and whom they will turn to when a difficulty appears.

How do you know whether buy-in is growing?

Three concrete signs.
1) The same people come back to the tool from one week to the next.
2) They mention it unprompted when they describe their work.
3) They suggest new uses themselves.

Conversely, use concentrated on one or two people, or vanishing as soon as the coaching stops, signals surface-level buy-in. These signals are picked up by observation and conversation, with no extra measurement tool.

What should you tell a leader who wants to go fast?
That speed comes from buy-in, not from haste.
A fast rollout to a team that has not bought in costs time twice: once to install it, once to relaunch it. A short pilot with a visible result convinces faster than a communication plan.

The right timetable is one where each step proves something to the next team.

What role do the first users play?

The first convinced users are the best relays. Their feedback, in their own words, carries more weight than a presentation from leadership. Identify them early, give them a little time to go deeper, and let them show colleagues what works and what does not.

Take care not to turn them into isolated experts: their role is to share, not to carry the use alone for the whole team.

How do you keep momentum after launch?
A use that is no longer animated fades. Plan a short, regular meeting to share what worked, what got in the way and what each person tried. Fifteen minutes is enough, provided it actually happens.

Highlight a concrete result rather than a usage figure: a task shortened, a file delivered earlier. That is what other teams will remember.

Every team has its own rhythm, and comparing one team with another rarely helps. What matters is that each one can see, in its own work, what has changed since the tool arrived, and that someone listens when it says what still gets in the way. That listening is what keeps the tool alive after the launch effect has faded.

Four attitudes towards a new tool

In every team, reactions fall into roughly the same pattern. Spotting them lets you support each person in their own way, instead of giving everyone the same speech.

I often see four attitudes:
the enthusiasts, who have already tried it and want to go faster;
the cautious, who are waiting for concrete results;
the sceptics, who doubt the value and say so;
the worried, who fear for their job or their skills, often without saying it.

The enthusiasts become ambassadors, as long as they are not left on their own. The cautious are won over by proof. The sceptics deserve a hearing: they often spot real flaws. The worried first need reassurance about their future.

Age is not the issue

You often hear that younger people take to AI more easily. In practice, buy-in depends far more on perceived usefulness than on generation.

An experienced employee who saves an hour on a tedious task adopts the tool very quickly. A young employee who cannot see what it is for turns away from it. Reasoning by age leads to poor training choices, and sometimes to hurtful blunders.

Words that reassure, words that worry

The words of the announcement weigh more than you think. “Replace”, “automate jobs”, “productivity gains”: even with no bad intent, people hear them as a threat.

“Lighten”, “free up time for”, “help you to” often describe the same reality, but from the point of view of the person doing the work. Choosing your words is not window dressing: it means recognising that the person opposite has something to lose, and taking that seriously.

Protect learning time

Many rollouts fail for a very simple reason: teams are asked to learn a tool on top of their work, without being given the time.

Set aside a short, regular slot, for example one hour a week during the first month. It changes everything. That protected time also sends a message: for the company, learning is part of the job, not an extra effort to squeeze into the evening.

A shared library of good prompts

Every user ends up finding the wordings that work. If they stay in people’s heads, the company keeps relearning the same thing.

A shared library, kept simple, sorts these prompts by task: preparing meeting notes, rewording a letter, summarising a tender. Everyone draws on it and adds to it. Newcomers get started faster, and those who found the best practices get recognised.

Should you reward usage?

Recognise it, yes. Pay for it, no. A bonus tied to the number of logins pushes people to use the tool for the figure, not for the result, and it distorts the indicators leadership looks at.

The recognition that works best is often the simplest: mention a success in a meeting, make someone the go-to person, give them time to help their colleagues. What motivates people over time is being seen as useful.

How do you welcome newcomers?

By putting AI tools into the onboarding path, just like business software. A new employee who discovers in their first week the uses in place, the prompt library and the rules to follow picks up good habits straight away.

A buddy who is already comfortable with these tools speeds things up even more. The newcomer learns the team’s real uses, not just the ones in a manual.

Show internal successes

Nothing convinces like a colleague. An example from the team next door, with its figures and its limits, speaks louder than an international study or a vendor demo.

A short monthly share, five minutes in a meeting, where someone shows what the tool has brought them, gradually builds a culture. Show the trials that did not work too: they prove people are allowed to get it wrong.

The right to say it does not work

Buy-in is not obedience. A team that can say a tool does not suit them, without being suspected of bad will, gives you precious information.

Set up a simple channel for this feedback, and answer it. Teams see that their opinion counts, and leadership avoids pushing a tool nobody really uses.

What role for HR?

A supporting role, often underestimated. HR can bring AI uses into annual reviews, spot training needs and identify people who are struggling before they drop out.

They can also watch a sensitive point: when a tool saves time, what happens to that time?
If it only turns into extra workload, buy-in crumbles. If it goes into more interesting tasks, buy-in grows.

Remote teams?

The rules are the same, with one extra risk: isolation. Alone in front of a screen, an employee who gets stuck gives up faster, with no neighbour to ask.

Short video sessions, pairs who help each other and a written space where people can ask questions without fear of looking behind make up for most of that distance.

When the tool changes?

AI tools change constantly: a new interface, a new version, a feature moved or removed. Every change can break a habit that has barely formed, and wipe out the benefit of the first weeks.

The go-to person plays a key role here: they follow the updates, warn the team about what is changing, update the prompt library and, if needed, offer a short refresher. A change that is announced is absorbed in a few days. A change that is imposed can make people drop the tool.

What do you do about lasting refusal?

Start with a one-to-one conversation, with no pressure. A refusal that lasts often hides a precise reason: a bad first experience, a task the tool does not serve, a worry about the future of the job.

Often, the tool is not essential for everyone, and you can give it time. When it becomes essential, for example because a whole process depends on it, explain it clearly, give more support and set a realistic deadline. Forcing it rarely produces real buy-in.

How long before a use becomes a habit?

Usually several weeks of regular practice. The first days are curiosity. The habit forms when the tool is used for a real task, several times a week.

Two checkpoints help you follow this shift: after one month, to fix what gets in the way, and after three months, to check that the use holds once the launch buzz has faded.

A typical example

Take an SME of forty people that bought licences for everyone, with no result after three months. Rather than adding a general training course, leadership starts by listening: a short conversation with each team brings up unspoken worries and two uses that already work.

It protects one hour a week, builds a prompt library from those two uses and asks the people who found them to present them. Three months later, regular use has clearly grown, without spending a penny more. What was missing was not the tool. It was the way of supporting people.

What buy-in really takes

Consideration more than conviction. You do not win a team’s buy-in by explaining that the tool is good. You win it by showing that you understand what it changes for them.

A budget buys licences. Only the attention you pay to people makes them want to use them.
