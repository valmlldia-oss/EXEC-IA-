The AI budget is not the issue.
Buy-in is.

September 25, 2026

Short version →

A generous AI budget does not guarantee that teams will use the tool. A HubSpot study of marketers shows a wide gap between company investment and employees’ appetite for using it. Three levers make the difference: explain before equipping, train on everyday uses, and prove a concrete result on a small scope before scaling up.

A generous budget approved without discussion. Teams that do not move at the same pace. I have seen this scenario in executive committees, long before AI became the topic.

A HubSpot study, which I came across in 2026, confirms it: 98% of companies planned to maintain or increase their AI investment in 2025. Only half of their employees (51%) wanted to use it.

The study covers marketers. Read it as a signal, not as a statistic that holds for every function. But the signal is clear: the budget is not the issue. Buy-in is.

🔑 Why does buy-in weigh more than budget?

Because a tool never changes an organisation on its own. Teams decide every day whether to use it or work around it.

Resistance almost never comes from the technology. It comes from the habits the tool is asked to replace.

What does the gap between investment and willingness to use the tool reveal?

Two clocks run in parallel. The first is the clock of the decision: a budget is voted in a few weeks, a tool is rolled out in a few months. The second is the clock of habits: they change at the pace of the teams, not of the executive committee’s calendar.

When these two clocks are not synchronised, the organisation spends money on a use that does not exist yet. The dashboard says the tool is deployed. The reality on the ground says it is opened from time to time, then forgotten.

Why is the budget spent a poor indicator?

Because it measures an intention, not a change. A budget spent proves that a decision was taken. It says nothing about what teams do differently on Monday morning compared with yesterday.

The right indicator is regular use: how many people use the tool each week, for which precise task, and what have they stopped doing by hand?

How do you measure buy-in, in practice? Three questions are enough to start.

  • Who uses it every week, and for what?
  • Who tried it and gave up, and why?
  • Which task has really disappeared or become shorter?

These answers come from a twenty-minute conversation with a few team members, not from a satisfaction form. They give a truer picture than any login statistic.

Why does a team adopt a tool?

A team adopts a tool when it sees the benefit for itself, not only for the company. The gain must be felt in daily work: a shorter task, one less error, a file prepared faster.

When the benefit is only visible on a leadership dashboard, the tool is experienced as an extra burden. The first job is therefore to translate the expected benefit into the language of the people who will use it.

Which forms of resistance come up most often? Three come back. The fear of getting it wrong, especially when the tool seems to leave no room for error. The fear of being replaced, which is rarely voiced openly. And the impression that the tool adds a step instead of removing one.

Each calls for a different answer: a right to try, a clear word from leadership about what will not change, and a demonstration on a real task rather than on a catalogue example.

Why explain before equipping?

🎯 Which levers really make the difference? Three levers, from the simplest to the most demanding: explain before equipping, train on real uses, prove early.

The why always comes before the tool. A team that understands what the tool changes in its daily work, and what it does not change, adopts it more readily than a team told that a rollout is coming.

How do you train without overwhelming teams? By starting from everyday uses, not from the list of features. One hour on a task a person does every week is worth more than a day of general demonstration.

When should the tool be extended to the whole organisation?

After proving a concrete result, early, on a limited scope. That result becomes the argument, in place of the promise. The rollout then reaches teams who have already seen what the tool delivers.

What should you do when a team works around the tool? Start by understanding, before restating the rule. A workaround is almost never ill will. It reveals a need the tool does not cover, a step that is too heavy, or a habit nobody helped to change.

A team that works around a tool is telling you, without saying it, what it expects from it. That is valuable information, provided you gather it without hunting for culprits.

What role does the leader play in buy-in?

A role of demonstration more than of decision. A leader who uses the tool personally, who says what it is useful for and where it disappointed, gives teams permission to try and to say what does not work.

Conversely, a tool imposed without being used by those who impose it sends a message that contradicts the speech.

Which mistakes should you avoid in a rollout? Four mistakes come back: deploying everywhere at once, training on the list of features, announcing without explaining the why, and judging success by the budget consumed rather than by real use.

Each has the same origin: treating the tool as a purchase to succeed rather than as a change of practice to support.

How do you run a thirty-day pilot?

Week one: choose a volunteer team and a task that recurs every week, then explain why. Week two: train on that task only. Week three: let the team use it and note what gets in the way. Week four: measure the result and decide to continue, adjust or stop.

At the end of the month, the leader has something the budget alone never provided: proof, inside their own organisation, of what the tool brings or does not bring.

What role should line managers play? A central one. They turn the decision into daily practice. A convinced manager sets the example, protects learning time and reports what gets in the way. A manager who was not involved becomes, unintentionally, the first brake.

Before rolling out, it is worth holding a short session for these relays: what changes for them, what they will tell their teams, and whom they will turn to when a difficulty appears.

How do you know whether buy-in is growing?

Three concrete signs: the same people come back to the tool from one week to the next, they mention it unprompted when they talk about their work, and they suggest new uses themselves.

Conversely, use concentrated on one or two people, or vanishing as soon as the coaching stops, signals surface-level buy-in. These signals are picked up by observation and conversation, with no extra measurement tool.

What should you tell a leader who wants to go fast? That speed is won through buy-in, not haste. A fast rollout to a team that has not bought in costs time twice: to install it, then to restart it. A short pilot with a visible result convinces faster than a communication plan.

The right timetable is one where each step proves something to the next team.

What role do the first users play?

The first convinced users are the best relays. Their feedback, in their own words, carries more weight than a presentation from leadership. Identify them early, give them a little time to go deeper, and let them show colleagues what works and what does not.

Take care not to turn them into isolated experts: their role is to share, not to carry the use alone for the whole team.

How do you keep momentum after launch? A use that is no longer animated fades. Plan a short, regular meeting to share what worked, what got in the way and what each person tried. Fifteen minutes is enough, provided it actually happens.

Highlight a concrete result rather than a usage figure: a task shortened, a file delivered earlier. That is what other teams will remember.

Every team has its own rhythm, and comparing one team with another rarely helps. What matters is that each one can see, in its own work, what has changed since the tool arrived, and that someone listens when it says what still gets in the way. That listening is what keeps the tool alive after the launch effect has faded.
