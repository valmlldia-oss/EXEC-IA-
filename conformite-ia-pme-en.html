AI Act and GDPR: what an SME
using AI actually has to do.

Checked on 24/09/2026 · Reading time: 9 min

Short version →

The EU AI Act applies to any SME that uses or provides an AI system, regardless of size. Only the level of obligation and the relief available change. Three blocks are already in force:

  • prohibited practices and AI literacy (understanding what AI does) since February 2025;
  • governance and general-purpose model obligations since August 2025;
  • transparency for systems that interact with people since August 2026.

The bulk of the high-risk obligations has been postponed to 2 December 2027 by the Digital Omnibus (in plain terms: an EU simplification text).

The GDPR applies in parallel whenever personal data is processed.

Most SMEs first encounter the AI Act through articles about multi-million-euro fines and "high-risk" systems, without ever spelling out what actually applies to a ten-, thirty- or hundred-person company. This piece sorts that out: what is mandatory today, what has been postponed, and what an SME should actually check, with the official source behind each point.

This content is informational and educational. It does not constitute legal advice: every company's situation deserves review by a specialised lawyer, particularly for any use touching recruitment, credit or sensitive data.

Is your SME covered by the AI Act?

Yes, systematically, as soon as it provides or uses an AI system on the European market. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 sets out no general exception based on company size: what size changes is the relief available (simplified documentation, reduced fees, priority access to regulatory sandboxes, see below), not whether the obligations exist in the first place.

Two notions matter above all:

  1. Are you a provider (you develop, or have developed, an AI system placed on the market)?
  2. Are you a deployer (you use, in your business, a system supplied by a third party)?

Almost every SME is a deployer: it uses an existing AI tool rather than building one. Deployer obligations are markedly lighter than provider obligations, except when the use case falls into the high-risk category.

The current timeline, updated on 24 September 2026

The timeline moved this year: the Digital Omnibus postponed a large share of the obligations.

Digital Omnibus, in plain terms: an EU text that simplifies several digital rules and pushed back the AI Act's high-risk obligations.
It is Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026, in force since 27 July 2026.

Here is the exact state of the deadlines, with the official source for each.

In force
  1. 1 August 2024

    Entry into force of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

  2. 2 February 2025

    Prohibited AI practices (Art. 5) and the AI literacy obligation for staff, meaning knowing what an AI tool can and cannot do (Art. 4)

  3. 2 August 2025

    Governance rules, obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models, penalty regime

  4. 2 August 2026

    Transparency for systems that interact with people: conversational agents, synthetic content, deepfakes (Art. 50)

Upcoming or postponed
  1. 2 December 2026

    Two new prohibited practices (Art. 5): non-consensual intimate deepfakes, AI-generated child sexual abuse material, introduced by amending Regulation (EU) 2026/1744

  2. 2 December 2027

    Full Annex III high-risk obligations (recruitment, credit, biometrics, critical infrastructure, education…), postponed from 2 August 2026

  3. 2 August 2028

    High-risk systems embedded in already-regulated products (Annex I: medical devices, machinery…)

Sources: consolidated text of the Regulation (EUR-Lex) and the European Commission: AI regulatory framework. The Digital Omnibus is the amending Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026. A nuance on Article 50: systems already placed on the market before 2 August 2026 have, for the marking of synthetic content only (Art. 50(2)), a transitional deadline until 2 December 2026 (Art. 111(4)); the other Article 50 transparency obligations remain applicable from 2 August 2026.

What an SME should actually do, starting now

  1. Check that no use case falls under a prohibited practice: including generalised social scoring, subliminal manipulation exploiting a vulnerability, biometric categorisation inferring political or religious opinions, emotion recognition at work outside medical or safety exceptions, and, from 2 December 2026, non-consensual intimate deepfakes and AI-generated child sexual abuse material. No size-based relief applies here.

  2. Take measures to develop AI literacy (understanding what an AI tool can and cannot do, and using it wisely) among the people who use AI or decide on AI use cases within the company, an obligation in force since February 2025, but an obligation of means: the text explicitly states it does not require guaranteeing any specific level. Documented internal awareness can be enough for an SME, depending on the use case.

  3. Clearly inform anyone interacting with an AI system (a conversational agent, generated content, a synthetic image) that they are dealing with AI, whenever this is not already obvious from context.

  4. Identify whether a use case falls into the high-risk category (recruitment, credit scoring, staff assessment…). Full deployer obligations apply from 2 December 2027, but the inventory of affected use cases is worth building now, not the day before the deadline.

GDPR and AI: the reflexes that apply in parallel

The AI Act governs the AI system itself; the GDPR governs the personal data it processes, independently of the risk level the AI Act assigns to it. A tool the AI Act treats as low-risk can still trigger a GDPR obligation if the data it handles is sensitive. Four reflexes apply as soon as an AI system touches personal data: an identified lawful basis before any processing (Article 6 GDPR), clear information for the people concerned, minimisation of the data used to configure or fine-tune the tool, and a data protection impact assessment as soon as the processing is likely to result in a high risk. The French data protection authority, the CNIL, has published dedicated AI guidance on this exact point. If the AI provider is established outside the European Union, transfer safeguards (standard contractual clauses) come on top of these four points.

What the AI Act specifically eases for SMEs and microenterprises

Four separate articles ease the burden for SMEs:

  • Article 11 opens simplified technical documentation, accepted by authorities, to every SME (and to small mid-cap companies), not only to microenterprises.
  • Article 62 sets out priority access to national regulatory sandboxes to test a system under supervised conditions, certification fees reduced in proportion to company size, and dedicated awareness and communication channels.
  • Article 99 caps an SME's fine at whichever is lower between the fixed amount and the percentage of turnover: for a prohibited practice, the general ceiling is 35 million euros or 7 % of global turnover. An SME is charged whichever of the two is lower, never the higher one.
  • SMEs and microenterprises without partner or linked enterprises also benefit, under Article 63, from simplified compliance with certain elements of the quality management system required for high-risk systems.

None of this relief removes a substantive obligation; it reduces the documentation burden and the cost of reaching compliance.

01Is a small company really covered by the AI Act?+

Yes, and this is the most common misunderstanding. The AI Act applies to any organisation that provides or uses an AI system on the European market, regardless of size. What size changes is not whether the rules apply, but the level of obligation resting on you and which relief measures you can claim. An SME using a third-party AI tool to screen job applications or assess credit risk is covered by deployer obligations, even if it never wrote a single line of AI code itself. The only question worth asking is not how small you are, but what you actually use AI for and which risk category it falls into.

02What is the difference between a provider and a deployer?+

A provider develops an AI system, or has one developed, and places it on the market under its own name; a deployer uses that system in the course of its professional activity. Almost every SME is a deployer: it buys or subscribes to an existing AI tool rather than building one. Deployer obligations are markedly lighter than provider obligations, except when the system used falls into the high-risk category (recruitment, credit scoring, biometrics...). Knowing which side of that line you sit on stops you from imposing provider-level obligations on yourself out of excess caution, and equally from underestimating a real deployer obligation.

03What is mandatory today, and what has been postponed?+

Three blocks are already in force: prohibited AI practices and the AI literacy obligation since February 2025, governance rules and obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models since August 2025, and transparency obligations for systems that interact with people (conversational agents, synthetic content) since August 2026. On 2 December 2026, two new prohibited practices are added (non-consensual intimate deepfakes, AI-generated child sexual abuse material), introduced by amending Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. The bulk of the Annex III high-risk obligations, originally due in August 2026, has been pushed back to 2 December 2027 by that same regulation, and high-risk systems embedded in already-regulated products (medical devices, machinery) now have until 2 August 2028. The timeline genuinely moved this year, this is not a rumour.

04Which AI practices are prohibited, regardless of company size?+

These bans admit no size exception. They cover, among others, and have been in force since February 2025: subliminal manipulation or exploiting vulnerabilities (age, disability, social or economic situation) to distort behaviour in a harmful way, generalised social scoring of a person based on their behaviour, biometric categorisation used to infer political opinions, religious beliefs or sexual orientation, and emotion recognition in the workplace outside strictly limited medical or safety exceptions. From 2 December 2026, two further bans are added by amending Regulation (EU) 2026/1744: non-consensual intimate deepfakes of an identifiable person, and AI-generated child sexual abuse material. An SME considering an AI use case touching recruitment, customer scoring or staff monitoring should check this point before any other compliance step. It is the one category that admits no size-based relief.

05Does the GDPR apply on top of the AI Act?+

Systematically, as soon as an AI system processes personal data, which covers the vast majority of business uses. The AI Act governs the system itself; the GDPR governs the data it processes, independently of the risk level the AI Act assigns to it. In practice this means an identified lawful basis before any processing, clear information for the people concerned, minimisation of the data used to configure or fine-tune the tool, and a data protection impact assessment as soon as the processing is likely to result in a high risk. A tool the AI Act treats as low-risk can still trigger a GDPR obligation if the data it processes is sensitive.

06What relief does the AI Act provide for SMEs and microenterprises?+

Four separate articles ease the burden for SMEs. Article 11 opens simplified technical documentation, accepted by authorities, to every SME, not only to microenterprises. Article 62 sets out priority access to national regulatory sandboxes, certification fees reduced in proportion to company size, and dedicated awareness measures. Article 99 caps an SME's fine at whichever is lower between the fixed amount and the percentage of turnover, never the higher one. SMEs and microenterprises without partner or linked enterprises also benefit, under Article 63, from simplified compliance with certain elements of the quality management system required for high-risk systems. This is not limited to microenterprises alone. None of this relief removes a substantive obligation; it reduces the documentation burden and the cost of reaching compliance.
