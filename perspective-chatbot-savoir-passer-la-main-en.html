Air France’s chatbot answers
30% of requests alone.
For the rest, it knows
when to hand over.

September 25, 2026

Short version →

Business leaders, heads of customer service: Louis, Air France’s AI assistant, handles 30% of customer requests on its own. The rest it passes to a human, and the customer does not have to repeat everything.

To me, this figure does not show progress in AI. It shows the boundary Air France has learned to draw.

Three signals help you sense the limit before you cross it. Knowing when to hand over is still a human reflex.

You run a company and you wonder how far to trust a chatbot with your customer service?
This piece is for you.
Take Louis, Air France’s AI assistant. It merged several generations of specialised chatbots (baggage, travel inspiration, strikes and delays) into a single assistant, active on WhatsApp and Messenger.

🤝 What does that 30% figure really say?

Today, Louis handles 30% of customer requests on its own. The rest goes to a human, and the customer does not need to ask everything again.

What strikes me about this figure?
It is not the AI that has progressed the most. It is the boundary Air France has learned to draw between the tool and the person.

This is not an isolated case. According to Salesforce, in 2025 customer service teams already hand an average of 30% of their cases to AI. They expect to reach 50% by 2027.
In other words, the boundary question is coming to almost every customer service team, SMEs included.

Automating a frequent request frees up time.
Knowing how to route the rest: that is the real skill.

Why does the handover matter as much as the automation?

Because your customers do not judge the technology. They judge their journey.
You can automate 30% of requests successfully and lose it all on a single failed handover: the one where the customer has to repeat everything to an adviser who knows nothing about the previous exchange.

A good handover passes on the context: what the customer asked, what the tool replied, what is left to solve. The adviser picks up the conversation where it stopped.
For the company, it is an organisational detail. For the customer, it is the whole difference between “someone is looking after me” and “I am starting from scratch”.

Which requests should never be automated end to end?

Those where the stakes are high or emotions run strong: a dispute, an emergency, a customer in difficulty, a decision that commits the company.
These requests are often few. But their effect on trust is huge.

For these cases, the best automation spots the situation and alerts a human fast.

What should you take from the Air France example?
That the result does not come from how sophisticated the tool is. It comes from how clear the roles are.
The assistant handles what is frequent and simple. People handle what calls for judgement, listening or a decision.

This split moves: you revisit it as the tool improves and teams see what it does well.

What can be automated without risk?

Frequent, factual requests with a stable answer: the status of an order, a timetable, a known rule, a document to find.
The right answer depends neither on the customer’s mood nor on a judgement call.

That is where automation frees up the most time for your teams. It is also where a mistake costs the least.

How does a customer experience a failed handover?
As an admission: the company did not organise itself for them.
They repeat their problem, they wait, they switch channels. They remember that moment more vividly than all the answers that worked.

Trust is rarely lost over a single mistake. It is lost when people feel they are not being heard, especially when it is urgent.

How do you sense the limit before crossing it?
To me, three signals are enough.
1) The customer repeats the question.
2) They cannot find a way to reach a human.
3) The tone does not match how serious the request is.

Does the customer repeat the question before getting a useful answer?

When the same question keeps coming back, the tool is not meeting the real need. That is the first signal: the conversation needs to change hands.

Is there a simple option to reach a human?
If no clear option appears, customers feel trapped.
Good automation makes the way out to a person visible and immediate. A button, a sentence, a phone number: the form does not matter, as long as nobody has to hunt for it.

Does the tone adapt to how serious the request is?

The tone cannot stay the same for a trivial question and for an emergency. A staff strike does not call for the same register as a baggage question.
AI handles the volume. Recognising the right moment to hand over is still a human reflex.

Who decides the limit between the tool and the person?
A named person, not a technical setting. The limit is a service decision: which requests the assistant handles alone, which it passes on, and on which signals.
It has an owner, it is written down, and it is reviewed.

Without an owner, the limit moves on its own, update after update, without anyone having chosen it.

How do you know the limit is in the right place?

By listening to two sources:

  • customers, through their feedback and repeated requests, and
  • advisers, who see what reaches them after the handover.

If advisers often receive misunderstood requests, the tool keeps control too long.
If they receive simple requests, it hands over too early.

The percentage of requests handled alone is a result to read with care: it matters less than the satisfaction of those who went through the tool.

Where do you start in your customer service?
With the most frequent and simplest requests: that is where automation frees up the most time, with the least risk.
At the same time, define from day one the signals that trigger a handover to a person. Then check that this handover is visible and quick.

Then widen gradually, always keeping the same rule:

  • the tool handles the volume,
  • the person handles what matters.

Which question should you ask your teams?

Just one: in which cases did your customer feel they were talking to someone who did not understand them?
The answers almost always draw the boundary the company has not drawn yet.

That is where the quality of the relationship is decided, far more than in the percentage of requests handled.

What should be passed on to the person taking over?
The full context: the initial request, the answers already given, the information the customer has already provided and the exact point where the exchange stalled.
The person should be able to reply without asking a single question already asked.

This summary is prepared in advance, with the teams who receive the requests, and tested regularly on real cases.

How do you involve frontline teams?

By giving them a voice on the boundary. Advisers know which requests recur, which get complicated and which are better handled in person. Their view avoids moving the limit blindly.

A regular review of a few real exchanges, with them, is enough to spot useful adjustments.

Which indicator should you follow first?
Less the share of requests handled alone than the satisfaction of those who went through the tool, and the time it takes to reach a person when they want one.
A high automation rate can hide real frustration.

The right goal is not to make human exchanges disappear but to reserve them for the moments where they matter most.

How do you test the set-up before rolling it out?

By replaying real requests, including the most delicate: a complaint, an emergency, a badly worded question. Observe at what point the tool should have handed over, and what the person receives at that moment.

A test of a few dozen exchanges, reviewed by advisers, teaches more than a successful demonstration on three carefully chosen cases.

What should you tell the customer so they trust the tool?
The truth: they are talking to an assistant, here is what it can do, and here is how to reach a person.
A customer who has been told accepts the tool’s limits better than one who finds out, mid-conversation, that they are not talking to a human.

Transparency is also a courtesy: it shows the company stands behind what it has put in place.

How do you prepare advisers to take over?

By teaching them to pick up without repeating questions already asked, and to recognise the tone of an already weary customer. Taking over a conversation is a professional skill in its own right, worth a short training.

Advisers must also know when to tell the team that a reason for handover keeps coming back, so the boundary can be adjusted.

How do you keep control as the tool evolves?
By treating each change as a boundary to decide, not as a technical update. Before widening the assistant’s scope, the question is simple: which new requests will the assistant handle alone, and who checks that this stays acceptable for the customer?

A regular review of a few real exchanges, at each change, protects the quality of the relationship.

Does an SME need a conversational assistant?

Not always. Before installing anything, I ask one question: how many identical requests does your team receive each week?

If they number in the dozens, a good FAQ page, an automatic email reply and a booking link often cover the essentials, for far less money. An assistant becomes useful when those repetitive requests genuinely eat into the team’s time.

How much does a badly set-up assistant cost?

More than you think, because the cost shows up on no invoice. Clients who give up without saying a word, advisers rescuing conversations that started badly, a brand image that suffers in online reviews.

You have to go looking for these costs: read a few real conversations regularly, track client reviews, ask your advisers. One hour a month is often enough to avoid months of silent decline.

How do you choose a vendor?

Beyond the demo, I look at six criteria:
does the tool answer only from sources you control?
does the handover to a person pass on the full context?
does the tool connect to your software, especially your customer relationship tool?
where is the data hosted, and how long is it kept?
can you easily review the conversation history?
can someone without technical skills update the information?

A vague answer on any of these points weighs more than a spectacular feature.

The quality of answers depends on what you give it

An assistant only knows what it has been taught. If your prices, lead times or conditions change and nobody updates its knowledge base, it answers with total confidence… using outdated information.

So you need a content owner, separate from the technical owner. Their job: keep what the tool uses up to date, and check with every rule change that the assistant takes it into account.

What should happen when the tool does not know?

It should say so. A well-configured assistant answers only from sources the company has validated. When a question falls outside that scope, it admits it.

An invented answer, even a plausible one, costs more than “I do not know, let me put you in touch with someone”. This instruction is written down in black and white during set-up, and tested with trick questions.

What the AI Act changes for assistants

From 2 August 2026, the European AI regulation imposes a transparency obligation: anyone interacting with an AI system must be told so, unless it is obvious from the context.

For a company, getting compliant is simple: a clear message at the start of the exchange, telling the client they are talking to an assistant and how to reach a person. It is also what builds trust.

Personal data in conversations

Clients often share more than you would imagine: a file number, an address, sometimes health or family details. These exchanges fall under the GDPR.

Three precautions:
keep conversations only as long as needed, with a retention period set in advance;
check where the vendor hosts the data and what it does with it;
never ask the client for more than their request requires.

One service across every channel

Website, email, WhatsApp, phone: clients move from one to another without thinking. If they get a different answer depending on the channel, they stop believing any of them.

The same information, the same rules for handing over to a person and the same tone must apply everywhere. To me, that is the real lesson from Air France: one single assistant, rather than a collection of tools that contradict each other.

Nights and weekends?

An assistant answers at any hour. Your teams do not. No need to hide it: organise what comes next.

When nobody is available, the tool can collect the request, sum up the key points and announce a realistic callback time. A client who knows they will be called back tomorrow at ten waits calmly. One going round in circles in a dead-end conversation does not.

The advisers’ job changes

This is an effect people often forget. When the assistant handles the simple requests, advisers only get the difficult cases: complaints, emergencies, clients who are already annoyed.

Their day becomes more demanding, emotionally and intellectually. Plan for it: training for tense situations, recovery time between two difficult exchanges, recognition of this new expertise. Otherwise, automation moves the fatigue around instead of reducing it.

Two more indicators to watch

Beyond satisfaction, two measures flag a problem quickly:
the recontact rate, meaning the share of clients who come back with the same request in the following days;
satisfaction measured right after a handover to a person.

A rising recontact rate means incomplete answers. Low satisfaction after a handover means a poorly prepared transfer. Both can be fixed, as long as you look at them.

Public complaints on social media?

They deserve a rule of their own. An unhappy public message is read by hundreds of people. One clumsy automatic reply, and you have a controversy.

My rule: a first human reply, short and personal, offering to continue in private. AI can help spot these messages and prepare a draft. But the public reply carries the company’s signature: it goes through a person, every time.

Requests in several languages?

Current tools understand and reply in many languages. You can serve foreign clients without hiring.

Stay careful on binding topics, though: one nuance mistranslated in a refund condition, and you have a dispute. In those cases, hand over to a person who masters the language.

Should the assistant have a first name?

Air France called its assistant Louis. A first name makes the exchange warmer and gives the service an identity. But it must not make the client believe they are talking to a person.

The right balance fits in one welcome sentence: a first name, a clear statement that it is an assistant, and a way to reach someone. Warmth does not rule out transparency. It makes it easier to accept.

A typical example

Take a service SME that gets around a hundred questions a week about its opening hours, lead times and file tracking. It hands these three topics to an assistant, fed by a knowledge base that a designated person keeps up to date.

Everything else, complaints, quotes, special cases, goes to the team with a summary of the exchange. After three months, the team has won back several hours a week, the recontact rate is stable, and clients know from the very first message that they can reach a person.

What the Air France example really teaches

You recognise a successful assistant by the moment it steps aside. Answering part of the requests alone is useful. Recognising the moment when a person must take over is what makes the difference.

It is a service decision as much as a technical setting. It belongs to leadership, because it shapes the relationship with every one of your clients.
